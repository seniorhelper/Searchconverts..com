Leak two

What a no-show really costs, and which number to believe

No-show statistics are among the most copied and least checked figures in healthcare. Before you benchmark yourself against one, it is worth knowing what each number actually measured.

No-show rate benchmarks by care setting with the source of each figure
Setting No-show rate Where it comes from
Urgent care and same-day models 2–8% Short lead time between booking and visit leaves less room to forget or change plans.
Well-run US private practices 5–7% MGMA benchmarks, with single-specialty groups at 6.81% in 2023.
Primary care 12–15% Reported across practice benchmarking sources.
Academic medical center clinics 18.8% Kheirkhah et al., BMC Health Services Research, ten clinics, range 12.6% to 25.7% by specialty.
Global outpatient average ~23% Systematic review of 105 studies, weighted toward academic, hospital and public-sector settings.
Safety-net and Medicaid-heavy clinics 25–30% Largely driven by transportation and scheduling access barriers.

Both the 5% and the 23% are correct. They measure different settings using different definitions. A private orthopedic group comparing itself to a global outpatient average will conclude it is doing brilliantly when it is not, and a community clinic comparing itself to MGMA will conclude it is failing when it is performing normally for its population.

The $150 billion figure deserves a warning label

You will see it everywhere: missed appointments cost the US healthcare system $150 billion a year. It traces back to a 2017 opinion piece written by a scheduling vendor's marketing executive, with no published methodology. It works as a statement of scale. It should not appear in a business case.

The figure that survives scrutiny is roughly $196 per missed appointment, measured across ten clinics in a peer-reviewed study, in 2008 dollars. For a surgical or specialist consult the downstream cost is considerably higher, because the missed visit delays a procedure rather than an office call. Use your own average visit revenue and you will get a better answer than either published number.

On charging no-show fees

Fees in the $25 to $50 range are increasingly common and they do reduce no-shows. The rules differ by payer: Medicare permits charging beneficiaries for missed appointments provided the practice bills all patients the same way, while Medicaid generally prohibits billing the patient. There is no CPT code for a no-show, so it is never billable to insurance.

The trade is real. A fee recovers a little revenue and can push a price-sensitive patient out of your practice entirely. Written policy, disclosed at scheduling and at check-in, applied consistently. Then measure whether your no-show rate and your new-patient volume both moved, because looking at one without the other is how practices talk themselves into a bad decision.

Most of the improvement available here is not the fee anyway. Multi-touch reminders, easy self-service rescheduling, a waitlist that fills gaps, and a confirmation call on high-value visits move the number further, and they do it without making anyone feel penalized. That work sits with marketing automation rather than with your front desk doing it by hand.

The part most agencies get wrong

Tracking, pixels and HIPAA: what actually changed in 2024

A federal court struck down part of the government's tracking guidance in June 2024. A lot of marketing agencies read that as permission to put the Meta Pixel back on everything. That reading is wrong, and it is expensive.

December 2022

OCR issues the tracking bulletin

The HHS Office for Civil Rights publishes guidance stating that regulated entities may not use tracking technologies in ways that disclose protected health information to vendors. Its most contested claim was that an IP address combined with a visit to a public webpage about a health condition could itself be individually identifiable health information.

March 2024

A revision that satisfied nobody

OCR updates the bulletin. The American Hospital Association describes the changes as cosmetic and argues the revised version carries the same defects as the original.

20 June 2024

The court vacates part of it

In American Hospital Association v. Becerra, Judge Mark Pittman of the Northern District of Texas holds that OCR exceeded its authority. The specific portion vacated is the one treating an IP address plus a visit to an unauthenticated public webpage as individually identifiable health information.

29 August 2024

HHS declines to appeal

The agency withdraws its notice of appeal, making the ruling final on that point.

Still standing

Everything the ruling did not touch

The guidance as it applies to authenticated pages remains in force. That covers patient portals, telehealth platforms and anything behind a login, where trackers can see appointment dates, record numbers, diagnoses and billing detail. The underlying HIPAA rules were never in question: a third party may not receive protected health information without a signed business associate agreement, and Google and Meta will not sign one for their advertising and analytics products.

Separately

Lawsuits and the FTC did not go away

Class actions against health systems over pixel use continue under privacy theories that do not depend on HIPAA at all, and the Federal Trade Commission has pursued tracking enforcement independently. One health system settled a pixel case for $12.225 million. A favorable HIPAA ruling is not a defense in those cases.

The practical position is narrower and more workable than either extreme. Conversion tracking on ordinary public marketing pages is defensible. Tracking that fires on a scheduling flow, an intake form, a symptom checker or anything behind a patient login is where practices get into trouble, because that is where identity meets health context.

What that means for your advertising is that you measure differently rather than not at all: server-side conversion tracking you control, call tracking that does not pass clinical detail, offline conversion imports keyed to internal identifiers, and analytics from a vendor that will sign a business associate agreement. Slightly more work, and it keeps the ad account and the compliance officer in the same room.

Leak three

Patients rule you out before they ever call

In healthcare the decision usually happens in private, on a phone, reading about you. Most of what determines whether the call happens at all is written by other people.

The 2026 Patient Choice Report found that 55% of patients have walked away from at least one provider based on what they read online, a jump of 15 percentage points in a single year. That is a majority of your market, and it is moving in the wrong direction fast.

The same research found 66% of patients say a provider's response to reviews influences their trust. Not the review. The response. An unanswered one-star review is not simply a bad review, it is evidence that nobody is minding the practice, and an unanswered five-star review is a missed opportunity to sound like a human being.

This is the cheapest work available in healthcare marketing and the most consistently neglected. A steady, systematic request for reviews after good visits, a real reply to every one within a few days, and accurate listings across the places patients actually look. No campaign, no budget, no agency required for the basics. If you do nothing else from this page, do that.

What the ad platforms will and will not let you do

Healthcare advertising is a restricted category almost everywhere. Personalized advertising rules limit targeting audiences by inferred health condition, some treatment categories require certification before you can run at all, and creative claims are held to a higher standard than in other industries. Telehealth, addiction treatment and pharmacy categories carry their own certification requirements that change periodically.

The practical consequence is that healthcare campaigns win on intent and geography rather than on audience targeting. Somebody searching for your procedure inside your catchment area is worth more than any behavioral segment you are not allowed to build. That is why paid search tends to carry more of the load in healthcare than in other industries, and why the landing page and the phone call matter more than the targeting does.

The engagement

What we actually do for a practice

In this order, because buying more calls for a phone that already misses a third of them is the most common way healthcare marketing money disappears.

Fix the phone first

Call routing, overflow coverage, after-hours handling and a documented recovery process for missed calls. The benchmark follow-up rate on missed opportunities is under 26%, which means most practices never call back the person who called them.

Train the people answering it

The conversation that turns an inquiry into a booked appointment is a skill, not a personality trait. Front-desk call handling is coachable and measurable, and it sits alongside our sales training work.

Search advertising built on intent

Procedure and condition searches inside your catchment, with landing pages that answer the question the search asked. Restricted-category rules and creative review handled as part of the build.

Booking that works at 11pm

A meaningful share of patients decide outside office hours. Online scheduling that actually reflects your availability captures them; a contact form that sits until Monday does not. Custom scheduling tools fall under web app development.

Reminders and recall

Multi-touch reminders to cut no-shows, plus recall for patients overdue for a return visit. Reactivating a lapsed patient costs a fraction of acquiring a new one and almost nobody does it systematically.

Reputation and listings

Review generation after good visits, replies to every review, and consistent information wherever patients look. Given that 55% have ruled out a provider over what they read, this is acquisition work, not admin.

Everything above is measured against booked, kept appointments rather than form fills or clicks. That is the same principle behind our work as a conversion rate optimization agency: the number that matters is the one at the end of the chain, and every stage before it is worth exactly what survives to that point.
