The curve is the important part and it takes a second to read. The gold dashed line is short-term effect, which falls as you move money away from activation. The rose dashed line is long-term effect, which rises as you invest in brand. The solid line is what they produce together, and it peaks near 60% brand rather than at either extreme.

That shape is why the answer is a split rather than a side. Pure activation wins this quarter and erodes the base that makes next year's activation cheap. Pure brand building starves the pipeline you need to survive long enough to benefit. Almost every argument between a performance marketer and a brand marketer is two people each describing one of those dashed lines.

What the curve is and is not. It is drawn to illustrate the published finding that combined short and long term profit growth peaks around a 60:40 split, not to compute your business precisely. Binet and Field's own work puts the optimum in a range that shifts by category and situation, from roughly 70:30 for a new entrant to 40:60 for an established brand in a mature market. Treat the peak as a direction of travel, and treat anyone offering a precise optimum for your company without seeing your data with suspicion.
The evidence base

Where these numbers come from

Marketing has more genuine research behind it than the industry's output suggests. Three bodies of work carry most of the weight on this page, and it is worth knowing what each one actually claims.

The 60:40 finding

Les Binet and Peter Field published The Long and the Short of It in 2013 for the Institute of Practitioners in Advertising, drawing on 996 case studies from the IPA Databank. The headline conclusion is that a roughly 60% brand and 40% activation split maximizes combined short and long term profit growth. The work has since prompted a large body of follow-up analysis and remains the most cited effectiveness benchmark in the field.

The nuance people skip: the optimum is not universal. It moves toward brand for new entrants and toward activation for established brands in mature categories, and it shifts by sector and objective. What holds across the dataset is the direction, which is that activation-only budgets underperform on any horizon longer than a quarter.

Excess share of voice

The second idea is older and simpler. Brands whose share of voice exceeds their share of market tend to grow, and brands whose share of voice sits below their market share tend to shrink. The gap between the two is excess share of voice.

Work by Danenberg, Kennedy, Beal and Sharp, published in the Journal of Advertising in 2016, puts the relationship at roughly half a point to seven tenths of a point of market share gained per year for every ten points of excess share of voice, with business-to-business results in the same region. That is a modest annual number that compounds, which is exactly why it is easy to abandon and expensive to have abandoned.

The gap between 7.8% and 9.0% is sampling, not error. Gartner surveys large enterprises. The CMO Survey covers a wider mix including smaller, growth-oriented firms, which consistently spend a higher share of revenue. If you are a small business, the higher figure is closer to your peer group, and both are averages that hide enormous variation by business model.

That variation matters more than the average. The CMO Survey's 2025 breakdown put business-to-consumer product companies at 15.5% of revenue against 6.4% for business-to-business product firms and 9.0% for business-to-business services. A single benchmark applied across those is worse than useless, because it will tell a consumer brand it is overspending and a B2B firm it is fine.

Two more numbers worth carrying into a budget meeting

Digital reached 61.1% of total marketing spend in Gartner's 2025 data, the highest share recorded since 2013, while traditional advertising stayed in negative growth. Paid media is the largest and only growing category in the Gartner series, at 31.4% of the marketing budget in 2026.

Meanwhile martech's share has fallen for five consecutive years, from 26.6% in 2021 to 19.4% in 2026, even as 62% of CMOs say they plan to invest more in it. Software is getting cheaper per unit of capability and budgets are being reallocated toward media and labor. If your tool stack has grown every year while your output has not, you are running against the trend for a reason worth examining.

The argument

Why allocation beats optimization

Improving a channel by 20% is hard, competitive work. Moving 20% of a budget from a weak allocation to a good one takes a decision, and it is usually worth more.

Consider the arithmetic. A team that spends a quarter fighting for a 15% improvement in cost per acquisition has done genuinely skilled work. A company spending nothing on demand creation in a category where buyers take months to decide has a structural problem that no cost-per-acquisition improvement addresses, because the pool of people who recognize the brand is not growing.

The pattern shows up most clearly over time. Activation-heavy budgets look excellent for four to six quarters. Efficiency metrics improve, attribution reports glow, and the case for cutting the unmeasurable work writes itself. Then acquisition costs start climbing, because the audience that already knew you has been harvested and the replacement audience was never built.

By the time that appears in the numbers it is usually diagnosed as a channel problem or a market problem. It is neither. It is an allocation decision made two years earlier, and it takes about as long to reverse as it took to create.

The three questions a plan has to answer

Everything else is implementation detail. Who exactly are you for, and who are you willing to be wrong for. What do you say that a competitor could not say about themselves. And how much are you willing to spend against how long you are willing to wait.

Most marketing plans we are asked to review answer none of those and then specify a posting schedule. The schedule is not the problem. The absence of a decision above it is what makes every subsequent choice arbitrary, which is why those plans feel busy and produce so little.

The work

What a marketing consulting engagement covers

Six areas. We rarely do all of them, because the value is in fixing the one that is actually wrong.

Budget level and split

What you spend against your peer group, and how it divides between building demand and capturing it. The first hour of nearly every engagement, and often the most valuable.

Positioning and message

Who you are for, what you say, and whether a competitor could put their logo on your website without changing a word. Uncomfortable to test and clarifying to fix.

Channel strategy

Which channels suit your buying cycle and margin rather than which are fashionable. We sell all of them, so the recommendation is not predetermined. See PPC management for the paid side.

Measurement that survives a long cycle

What to count, what to ignore, and how to judge work whose payoff arrives after the reporting period ends. Long-cycle attribution is covered in depth on our B2B marketing page.

In-house, agency or hybrid

What to build internally, what to buy, and what to stop doing. Frequently the recommendation is fewer moving parts rather than more capability.

The plan itself

A short written plan with a budget, a split, named channels, a measurement scheme and a review date. Short enough that people read it and specific enough to argue with.

Where the diagnosis lands outside marketing entirely, that becomes business consulting, and if the constraint is the sales conversation it becomes sales training. We would rather move you to the right work than run the engagement you asked for.

Marketing consulting questions, answered with sources

They make the decisions that sit above execution: how much to spend, how to split it between building demand and capturing it, who you are targeting, what you say, which channels suit your buying cycle, and how you will judge whether it worked. The distinction from an agency is that a consultant should be indifferent to which channel wins. In practice many firms are not, which is why it is worth asking what a consultant sells before you weigh their channel advice.

Activation reaches people who are ready to buy now and produces a measurable response quickly. Search advertising, retargeting and promotions are typical. Brand building reaches people who are not in the market yet, so that when they enter it they already recognize and prefer you. The effects behave differently over time: activation spikes and decays, while brand accumulates slowly and decays slowly. That difference in timing is the entire reason the two get measured unfairly against each other, because only one of them fits inside a monthly report.

It is the gap between your share of the marketing noise in a category and your share of the sales. Brands whose share of voice exceeds their market share tend to grow, and brands below it tend to shrink. It is a small annual number that compounds, which makes it easy to cut and expensive to have cut.

As a direction rather than a target, yes. The common small business pattern is close to 100% activation, which produces good numbers for a year or two and then rising acquisition costs as the audience that already knew you gets exhausted. Excess share of voice also does not require a large absolute budget, only spending more than your current market share implies, which in a local or niche market is achievable at modest sums. In an early launch phase a heavier activation weighting is reasonable, because a brand with no cash flow does not get to have a long term.

Not with last-click attribution, which will always credit the activation touch nearest the conversion. Use different instruments for different jobs: track market-level indicators such as branded search volume, direct traffic, prompted and unprompted awareness, and the share of inbound inquiries that already know your name. Ask buyers directly how they heard about you on forms and calls. Then judge activation on efficiency and brand on those leading indicators. Applying one measurement scheme to both guarantees you cut the half that cannot defend itself in a monthly report.
