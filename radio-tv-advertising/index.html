Most radio budgets buy presence when they needed frequency

Twelve spots a week spread across three stations feels like a campaign and behaves like background noise. The Radio Advertising Bureau's own guidance is 35 to 60 spots weekly on a single station to achieve effective reach. Almost nobody buys that way, which is why almost nobody rates broadcast.

Time-sensitive

If you are planning Q3 or Q4 of 2026, read this first

Political advertising is about to reprice your market, and local advertisers who have not planned for it will pay considerably more for worse placement.

AdImpact projects total political ad spending for this cycle at $10.8 billion, more than a 20% increase over the 2022 midterms and the most expensive non-presidential cycle in United States history. BIA Advisory Services puts local political spending alone at $8.4 billion.

That money lands on finite inventory. Industry reporting suggests radio rates rise roughly 10 to 20% on average in the weeks before an election, with surges of up to 50% in battleground states and on news and talk formats. Radio Ink has reported that CPMs could rise by as much as 80% in key markets during this cycle. Broadcast television absorbs the largest share of political spend and gets squeezed harder still.

There is a second problem beyond price. Political buyers frequently hold preemption rights that commercial advertisers do not, which means your fixed-position spot can be bumped for a candidate's, sometimes at short notice.

What to actually do about it

Buy early and lock rates before the crush, or deliberately sit out the four to six weeks around the election and put that money into channels political money does not touch. In a battleground state, the second option is frequently the better arithmetic.

Where you do stay on air, avoid news and talk formats during the peak, since that is where the competition and the listener fatigue concentrate. Music formats and off-peak dayparts stay comparatively affordable, and your ad has considerably less to compete against for attention.

The upside nobody mentions. Immediately after an election, inventory frees up and demand collapses. The weeks following the first Tuesday in November are historically one of the better buying windows in broadcast, and a business willing to shift a campaign by a month can often buy the same reach for meaningfully less.

Two structural things about buying broadcast that nobody tells a first-time advertiser. A station sales representative works for the station, not for you, and will never volunteer that a competing outlet reaches your audience better for less. And rate cards exist to be negotiated from, so paying the published rate marks you as inexperienced before the campaign even starts.

Also worth knowing: remnant and preemptible inventory costs far less than fixed position, with the trade-off that your spot can be bumped. For brand-building that is often an acceptable risk. For a time-sensitive promotion it is not.

Read the numbers carefully

Broadcast has the least trustworthy published statistics in marketing

We sell this channel and we are going to spend a section undermining the numbers used to sell it, because you will encounter them and they do not hold up evenly.

The reach figures are solid

Nielsen's Q3 2025 Comparable Metrics Report puts AM/FM radio at 84% weekly reach among adults 18 to 49, against 52% for linear television. Other Nielsen data reported through the Radio Advertising Bureau puts weekly reach at 87% of adults 18 and over. Some vendor pages claim 91%. The 84 to 87 range is well sourced, and anyone quoting a higher number should be asked which report it came from.

The in-car dominance is also real and matters commercially. More than 80% of ad-supported audio time in vehicles belongs to AM/FM, and roughly three-quarters of out-of-home weekday drive-time listening happens in a car. If your customers drive to work, that is a genuinely captive audience.

The return-on-investment figures are not

Here the sources diverge to a degree that should stop you. Nielsen's 2026 Audio Today data, as reported by Radio Ink, puts AM/FM's return at roughly two dollars for every dollar spent. Meanwhile statistics aggregators circulate claims of nine dollars per dollar and five-to-one sales lifts, with no visible methodology.

Those are not compatible. A gap that wide is not measurement noise, it is one set of numbers being repeated because it flatters the channel. We plan against the two-to-one figure, and if a campaign beats it, excellent.

The measurement ruler changed, and CPMs moved on paper

This one is genuinely technical and almost nobody explains it. Starting with the January 2025 survey, Nielsen changed its Portable People Meter markets from a five-minute listening qualifier to a three-minute one. Initial impact data showed average quarter-hour audiences rising roughly 24% across those markets.

Think about what that does. If reported audience jumps 24% while station rates stay flat, the apparent CPM falls by about a fifth and radio looks cheaper without anything actually changing. When comparing a 2024 CPM to a 2026 one in a metered market, you need to know whether you are comparing efficiency or comparing rulers.

How we actually measure broadcast. Not by the station's affidavit and not by an attribution model that cannot see radio at all. A dedicated phone number per station, web traffic and branded search lift during and after flights, and where budget allows a matched-market test: run the campaign in one market, hold out a comparable one, and compare. It is the only method that separates what the advertising caused from what the season did.

The television question

Broadcast TV, connected TV, and which one you actually want

These get discussed as one budget line and they behave nothing alike. The decision usually comes down to whether you need mass local reach or targeted reach, and how much production you can afford.

Broadcast and cable television

Still the way to reach a whole local market at once, particularly during news and live sport where viewing concentrates and time-shifting is impossible. It is also the most exposed to political displacement in an election year, and it carries real production costs that radio does not.

Linear TV's weekly reach among adults 18 to 49 is now roughly 52% against radio's 84%, which reframes the comparison. Television buys attention more completely when it lands, and lands with far fewer people than it used to.

Connected television

The same living-room screen with digital targeting and digital reporting attached. You can buy by geography, household characteristics and behavior, and you get completion rates rather than estimates. It costs more per thousand than linear and wastes far less. We cover it properly on our CTV advertising page.

How we usually recommend combining them

Radio for frequency, because it is the cheapest way to be heard repeatedly. Connected television for targeting and for the emotional weight that picture and sound together provide. Broadcast television when a specific local moment justifies it, such as a live event your customers are watching.

Creative matters more here than in any channel we sell. A radio spot has one job, which is to be understood by somebody driving who is not paying attention, and that means one idea, a phone number or web address repeated, and enough spots for repetition to do its work. Production sits with video production for anything filmed.

The work

What we do on a broadcast campaign

Buying is a third of it. The other two thirds are deciding whether to buy at all and proving afterward whether it worked.

The frequency check first

Whether your budget can achieve effective frequency in your market. If it cannot, we say so and point you somewhere the money works harder. That conversation happens before anyone opens a rate card.

Station selection and negotiation

Comparing offers across competing outlets rather than accepting one station's proposal, and negotiating from the rate card rather than to it. This is where most of the value in a media buy lives.

Schedule and daypart planning

Concentrated on the station and daypart your customers actually occupy, with enough weight to register. Including the decision to sit out expensive weeks rather than buy them thin.

Creative that survives inattention

One idea, said clearly, with the contact detail repeated. Radio is heard by people doing something else, which is a real constraint and the reason clever spots underperform plain ones.

Tracking built in before launch

A dedicated number per station, a landing page that matches the spot, and baseline measurement taken before the flight starts. See conversion optimization for what happens after the call connects.

Matched-market testing

Where budget supports it, running one market and holding a comparable one back. The only method that cleanly separates campaign effect from seasonality.

Broadcast works best as part of a mix rather than alone. Pairing a radio flight with search coverage means the person who hears the spot finds you when they look you up, which is PPC management work. The same logic applies to direct mail landing in the same weeks, and the combined planning question belongs with marketing consulting.

Whatever the channel, we count what survives to the end of the chain rather than what a station reports as delivered. That is the discipline behind everything we do as a conversion rate optimization agency, and broadcast needs it more than most, because it is the channel where nobody checks.

Honest scoping

When broadcast is right, and when it is not

Worth considering if

  • Your customers are defined by geography rather than by narrow interests
  • Your budget can sustain 35-plus spots weekly on one station for several weeks
  • You sell something with broad appeal in your area
  • Your customers commute, which is where radio listening concentrates
  • Digital costs in your category have risen to where broadcast CPMs look sane
  • You need to be known rather than merely findable
  • You can commit to a flight rather than testing a single week

Do not buy it if

  • Your budget only stretches to a handful of spots, which buys nothing
  • You sell to a narrow niche that broadcast cannot target
  • Nobody will answer the phone during the hours your spots run
  • You need to prove return within two weeks
  • You are in a battleground market planning to buy through the election peak
  • You want to spread the budget across several stations to feel safer
  • There is no tracking number and no plan to add one
