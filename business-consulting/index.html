The logic is deliberately simple. If you can only deliver 30 jobs and you are already selling 21, more advertising buys you a waiting list and some angry reviews. If you reach 40% of your inquiries, you have already paid for the other 60% and thrown them away. If your margin is thin, volume multiplies the problem rather than solving it.

This is a rough model, not an audit. It ignores seasonality, mix, fixed costs and the hundred specifics that make your business yours. What it does well is point at the right question, which is more than most engagements manage in their first month.

Why an agency publishes a tool that often says do not buy advertising. Because selling media to a business whose constraint is delivery capacity produces a bad outcome and a short relationship. We would rather find that out in a free thirty-minute call than four months into a contract. It costs us some sales and it has never once cost us a client.
What the data says

Businesses rarely fail for the reason the owner names

Bureau of Labor Statistics data puts first-year failure at about 20%, rising to roughly 49% by year five and 65% by year ten. Those are averages across all registered businesses, not the frequently repeated 90% figure, which applies mainly to venture-backed technology startups.

More useful than the survival rate is the cause. CB Insights analyzed 483 shutdown post-mortems, and the pattern is consistent enough to plan around.

Cited reasons for failure

From CB Insights' analysis of 483 post-mortems. Percentages exceed 100 because most closures cite several causes at once, which is itself the point.

Read that list again and notice what is missing. Not enough traffic is not on it. Insufficient advertising is not on it. The largest single cause is selling something the market does not want enough of, which no campaign fixes and most campaigns disguise for a while.

Cash is the other half of the story, and it behaves differently from everything else. SCORE, using US Bank research, puts cash flow problems as a factor in 82% of small business failures. Cash is usually the proximate cause while something else is the root cause. A business can be profitable on paper and still close, because payroll happens on Friday and the invoice clears in March.

The statistic that should worry you most

About a third of closures happen while the owner still believes the business is profitable or breaking even.

That is not a failure of effort. It is a failure of measurement. Profit on a monthly statement is not the same as cash in an account, gross margin is not the same as net, and revenue growth funded by lengthening payment terms looks identical to real growth right up until it does not.

It is also why we start every engagement with numbers rather than ideas. Not because the numbers are interesting, but because roughly one owner in three is operating on a picture of their business that is wrong in a direction that eventually ends the business.

The work

What business consulting actually covers here

Six areas, chosen because they are where the constraint usually turns out to live. We do not do all of them for every client. We do the one that is binding.

Constraint diagnosis

The first engagement, always. Where throughput is actually limited, what it costs you a month, and whether the fix is cheap or structural. Usually takes days rather than weeks.

Pricing and margin

The fastest lever in most small businesses and the least examined. Where you are underpriced, where discounting is habitual, and what a few points of margin is worth against the volume it would take to match it.

Process and protocol

Business process consulting in the practical sense: written quoting standards, follow-up cadence, handoffs that stop losing people, and the small number of things worth measuring weekly.

Cash and working capital

Deposit structures, payment terms, receivables discipline and the timing gap between doing the work and being paid for it. Not accounting, which your accountant should do, but the operating decisions that create the gap.

Capacity and delivery

What to fix before adding demand. Scheduling, utilization, the bottleneck workstation or person, and whether growth requires hiring or just sequencing the work differently.

The demand side, when it is the answer

Sometimes the constraint genuinely is at the top. When it is, we can build that too, which is the rest of this site. See marketing consulting for the planning end of it.

Two things we do not do. We are not accountants, lawyers or financial advisors, and where a question belongs to one of those professions we will say so rather than improvise. And we do not produce large strategy documents. A recommendation nobody executes is worth exactly nothing, which is the standard failure of the consulting industry and not one we intend to reproduce.

Where the constraint turns out to be the sales conversation itself, that becomes sales training rather than consulting. Where it is measurement and conversion, it becomes conversion optimization. Same diagnosis, different repair.

How an engagement runs

Four steps, and you can stop after any of them

Everything is quoted after the first conversation, because a pricing review and a full operational rebuild are not the same job and should not carry the same number.

A free call, with actual numbers

Thirty minutes. Bring rough figures on inquiries, close rate, capacity, average sale and margin. We will tell you what we think the constraint is and whether it is worth paying anyone to fix. A meaningful share of these calls end with a suggestion and no proposal, which is a feature.

The diagnostic

A paid, time-boxed look at the real numbers rather than the remembered ones. What throughput actually is at each stage, where the money leaks, and what each fix is worth annually. You get a short document with a ranked list, not a bound deck.

Fix the binding constraint

One thing at a time, in order of value. Working alongside your people rather than around them, because the changes have to survive our departure. Most first fixes are unglamorous: a pricing change, a follow-up rule, a scheduling adjustment.

Re-measure and move to the next one

The constraint always moves. Fix delivery capacity and the limit becomes demand. Fix demand and it becomes cash. This is why one-off strategy work disappoints: the map is accurate for about a quarter. Ongoing work is optional and plenty of clients stop here.

Field notes

The five constraints we find most often

After enough diagnostics the same handful keep appearing. Here is what each one looks like from the inside, and what a business consultant should actually do about it. You may recognize yours before you ever call anyone.

Common business constraints, how they present, and the usual first fix
Constraint How it presents Usual first move
Follow-up Plenty of inquiries, a full voicemail box, and a vague sense that leads are poor quality A written response standard and someone accountable for it. Costs nothing and usually returns the most.
Pricing Busy constantly, margins thin, prices unchanged for years while costs rose Reprice the next quote, not the whole book. Test on new work before touching existing customers.
Capacity Long lead times, cancellations, quality slipping, staff burning out Sequence differently before hiring. Then raise price, which is the only lever that fixes demand and margin at once.
Cash timing Profitable on paper, tense every payroll, always waiting on invoices Deposits and progress billing. Changing terms on new work is faster than chasing old receivables.
The owner Every decision routes through one person who is also the best salesperson Write down the three decisions that genuinely need you, then delegate the rest with a rule rather than a hope.

Why the order matters more than the list

Every item above is worth fixing. Doing them in the wrong sequence wastes most of the effort.

Fix demand while capacity is binding and you create a waiting list and a reputation problem. Fix capacity while margin is thin and you have simply bought the right to lose money faster. Hire before writing down how the work is done and you have added a person to an undefined process, which reliably produces two people doing it differently.

The sequence is usually: make each unit profitable, then make sure nothing leaks between stages, then add volume. Almost every business we see wants to start at the third step, because it is the only one that feels like growth.

What the constraint moving actually feels like

Expect the win to be short-lived, and treat that as success rather than failure. Relieve the tightest link and throughput rises until the next link becomes binding, usually within a quarter or two. This surprises owners who expected one fix to hold permanently.

It is also why we are skeptical of engagements sold as a single transformation. The map you buy today is accurate for a few months. What is durable is the habit of asking which link is narrow now, and a small set of numbers reviewed often enough to answer it. That habit is the actual deliverable, and it is the same discipline we apply as a conversion rate optimization agency: measure the end of the chain, fix the narrow part, measure again.

One caveat on all of this. Constraint thinking is a useful lens, not a law of physics. Some businesses have two binding limits at once, some have a problem that is genuinely strategic rather than operational, and occasionally the honest finding is that the market is too small to support the business at its current cost base. A consultant who never reaches that conclusion is not looking hard enough.
Honest scoping

When to call us, and when not to

Call us if

  • Revenue is growing and the bank balance is not
  • You are busy and cannot say which work is actually profitable
  • You have spent on marketing and cannot trace what it produced
  • You are about to hire, buy equipment or open a second location
  • You are the bottleneck and every decision still routes through you
  • Your prices have not changed in two years while your costs have
  • You suspect the problem is somewhere you have not looked

Do not call us if

  • You need accounting, tax, legal or financial advice, which are other professions
  • You want a strategy document rather than a change in the numbers
  • You are looking for a turnaround specialist for an insolvent business
  • You want validation for a decision you have already made
  • Nobody internally will have time to implement anything
  • You want a large branded consultancy for procurement or board reasons
  • The honest answer might be that the business model does not work, and you do not want to hear it

Questions we get

Business consulting questions, answered plainly

Business consulting services are outside help with diagnosing and fixing what limits a company's performance. In practice that covers pricing and margin, operating processes, cash and working capital, delivery capacity, and demand. The useful version starts with diagnosis rather than a predetermined solution, because the problem an owner presents is often not the one constraining the business. Our engagements begin with finding the binding constraint and stating plainly what fixing it is worth, before anyone proposes work.

Follow the chain and find the narrowest link. Inquiries, how many you actually reach, how many you close, how many you can deliver, what margin each carries, and how fast you get paid. Throughput is set by the tightest of those, so widening any other stage changes nothing. The calculator on this page runs that logic on your numbers. If you can deliver 30 jobs a month and you are selling 21, more advertising buys a waiting list rather than revenue.

For most small businesses this is not a software project. It is a small number of written rules that stop good people improvising differently each time, and it usually produces more than any new tool would.

Almost always pricing first, and the arithmetic is not close. A few points of margin flow straight to the bottom line with no additional delivery cost, while equivalent revenue from new customers requires acquisition spend, capacity and time. Pricing is also the least examined lever in most small businesses, because raising prices feels risky and running an ad feels productive. We look at pricing early in nearly every engagement, and it is frequently where the first real money is found.
