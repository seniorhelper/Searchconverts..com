Work out the response rate you need before you buy the list

Direct mail is the only channel where you commit the entire budget before a single person sees the creative. So the arithmetic has to happen first, and the only number that matters is what it costs to acquire a customer, not what percentage replied.

The whole calculation, in one line:
break-even response % = cost per piece ÷ (close rate × first sale × margin × LTV multiple)
Everything else on this page is context for those five inputs.

Notice what the formula does not contain: any industry average. Benchmarks tell you what is achievable, not what you need, and those are completely different questions. A business with a $12,000 average job and a 60% margin can profit at a fraction of a percent response. A business selling a $40 product cannot make mail work at almost any realistic response rate, and no amount of creative fixes that.

The lifetime value slider is where most campaigns are wrongly killed. If you judge mail on the first transaction and your customers come back four times, you have understated the return by roughly four hundred percent and you will cancel a channel that was working.

Before you trust any number

Nearly every direct mail statistic is published by someone who sells direct mail

This is not a small caveat. Search the topic and you will find the same handful of figures repeated across dozens of pages, almost all of them printers, mail houses or mailing software companies, each citing the others in a loop.

We sell direct mail too, so treat this page with the same suspicion. The difference we can offer is showing our working and telling you where the numbers conflict.

The one source with a real methodology

The ANA/DMA Response Rate Report is the closest thing this industry has to a neutral benchmark, and its most recent edition puts the average direct mail response rate at 4.4%. That figure blends two very different things, which is why quoting it alone is misleading.

Where the sources openly disagree

Look at house list performance and you will find some sources reporting 5% to 9% and others reporting 2.7% to 4.4% for the same category. That is not a rounding difference, it is a doubling. Prospect list figures range from 1% to 4.4% depending on who is publishing.

We are not going to pretend to resolve that. What we will say is that the honest planning move is to use the pessimistic end of every range, check whether the campaign still clears break-even, and only then run it.

Direct mail response rate ranges by audience type
Audience type Reported range What it actually measures
House list 2.7–9% Existing customers, past buyers, prior inquiries. The wide range reflects genuine disagreement between sources. This is measuring relationship depth more than mail effectiveness.
Prospect list 1–4.4% Rented or purchased names with no prior relationship. Performance here is mostly a function of list fit rather than creative.
EDDM saturation 1–2.5% Every door on a carrier route, addressed to a resident rather than a person. Cheapest postage, no targeting, lowest rates.
Blended average 4.4% The ANA headline figure. Mixing house and prospect lists makes this number nearly useless for planning a specific campaign.
On the famous email comparison. You will see direct mail described as roughly 37 times more effective than email, from a 4.4% response rate against email's 0.12%. The arithmetic is accurate and the conclusion people draw from it usually is not. Mail costs perhaps a hundred times more per contact than email does. Response rate per piece is the wrong comparison; cost per acquisition is the right one, and on that measure the two channels are far closer and frequently favor email for anyone who already has a list. The correct use of that statistic is to justify testing mail, not to replace email with it.
What it costs

Formats, postage and where the money goes

Direct mail pricing is unusually transparent, because postage is published by the Postal Service and printing is a commodity. The variable that actually moves your result is the list.

Direct mail formats and typical all-in costs
Format or line item Typical cost Notes
Oversized postcard $0.40–$1.00 The usual starting point. No envelope to open, so the message is seen whether or not anyone chooses to read it.
Letter package $1.00–$3.00 Higher cost, more room to persuade, and consistently among the stronger performing formats alongside oversized postcards.
Dimensional mailer $5.00–$25.00 Something with bulk to it. Only viable when one conversion carries serious revenue, typically business-to-business or major donors.
EDDM postage 25.9¢ Per piece through a business mail entry unit. Every address on a route, no names required.
Marketing Mail letter postage $0.467 For addressed mail to a list you hold or rent.
First Class postcard postage $0.65 Faster and forwardable, with address correction. Worth the premium on a house list you care about keeping clean.
List rental $0.05–$0.15 Per name for a targeted prospect list. Free if you are mailing your own customers, which is also where response rates are highest.

Two cost levers most people miss. Presorting and commingling reduce postage meaningfully, commonly cited in the range of 15% to 30%, and that saving can fund either more volume or better paper. And a clean address file avoids paying to mail people who moved, which sounds obvious and is routinely skipped.

The larger point is that cost per piece is the wrong thing to optimize. Halving your cost per piece by dropping to the cheapest possible format and the broadest possible list will usually cost you more per customer, because response rate falls faster than price does.

Why physical mail still works at all

Two reasons, one boring and one interesting.

The boring one is scarcity. Advertising mail volume has declined for years while inboxes have filled up, so the mailbox is now a less contested channel than it was. Attention is cheaper where competition is thinner, which is a supply argument rather than a claim about the medium.

The interesting one is processing. Neuroscience research from Temple University found that physical media produced more emotional processing, stronger recall and a higher willingness to pay than digital equivalents, and separate work commissioned through Canada Post found physical mail required less cognitive effort to process and recall. Those findings are real, and they are also frequently overstated by people selling paper. They explain why a well-made mailer can outperform its cost, not why any mailer will.

Measurement

Mail is trackable, and most campaigns are not tracked

The old complaint that you cannot measure direct mail has not been true for years. What is true is that measurement has to be built in before the piece prints, because you cannot add it afterward.

A dedicated phone number

The single most valuable tracking device in mail, because most responses to a local offer are calls. One number per campaign, forwarding to your real line, recorded and counted.

A QR code to its own landing page

Not your homepage. A page that matches the offer on the card, so the visit is attributable and the message does not break on arrival. That is conversion optimization work as much as mail work.

Personalized URLs

A unique web address per recipient. More setup, and it tells you exactly who responded rather than how many, which changes what you can do next.

Intelligent Mail barcodes

Delivery scan data telling you roughly when pieces land, so you can time your phone coverage and any supporting digital spend to the same week.

A holdout group

Withhold a random slice of the list and compare. The only method that separates what mail caused from what would have happened anyway, and almost nobody bothers.

Matched digital follow-up

Running search and social to the same audience in the same window, so the person who saw the card also sees you when they look you up. Detail on PPC management.

The unique code trap. Putting an offer code on the card and counting redemptions undercounts badly, because many people respond without ever mentioning it. If your only measurement is code redemption, expect to see perhaps half the true response and to conclude the campaign failed. Call tracking plus a holdout group gives a far more honest picture.
The work

What we do on a direct mail campaign

In this order, and we will stop at step one if the arithmetic says stop.

The break-even check

Before design, before list, before anything. If your economics require a response rate above the top of the published ranges, the honest answer is that mail is the wrong channel and we say so.

List strategy

Your own customers first, because that is where response rates are highest and the names are free. Then lookalike prospects, then saturation routes, in that order of expected return.

The offer

The single biggest lever in mail, ahead of design and well ahead of paper stock. A clear, time-bound, genuinely valuable offer beats a beautiful card with a vague one every time.

Design and copy

Built for a three-second decision at a recycling bin. Legible, specific, and matched to the landing page it points at. Wider identity work sits under branding and design.

Print, postage and drop timing

Format selection, postal optimization, and coordinating the drop with staffing so the phone gets answered during the week the mail lands.

Tracking and the second round

Call tracking, a matched landing page, a holdout group where the list is big enough, and then a decision about repeating that is based on cost per customer rather than on how the card looked.

Mail works best as one layer rather than a standalone channel. Pairing a drop with search coverage, so the person who reads the card finds you when they look you up, consistently outperforms either alone. Where the constraint turns out to be your existing customer list rather than new names, the higher-return work is usually email marketing and marketing automation, which cost a fraction of postage.

Two industries where we see mail perform particularly well: real estate, where farming a neighborhood is still effective and the transaction value is enormous, and dental practices targeting new movers within a tight radius. Both have the economics that make the arithmetic work.

Honest scoping

When mail is right, and when it is not

Mail is worth testing if

  • Your average sale is high enough that a fraction of a percent pays for the drop
  • You have a customer list you have never mailed
  • Your market is geographically tight, which is where saturation mail is cheap
  • Customers come back, so lifetime value carries the first acquisition
  • Your competitors have all moved entirely to digital
  • You are reaching an audience that is hard to target online
  • You have a genuinely good offer rather than a general awareness goal

Do not mail if

  • Your break-even response rate lands above the house list range
  • The product is low-value and customers do not repeat
  • Nobody will answer the phone in the week the mail arrives
  • There is no offer, just a logo and a list of services
  • You want it measured by an offer code alone
  • Your audience is national and thinly spread, where postage scales badly
  • You need results this week, since print and delivery take longer than that

Repeated contact to a well-chosen list builds familiarity and catches people whose timing shifts. The practical approach is to plan three drops to a smaller list rather than one drop to a list three times the size, and to judge the program on the whole sequence rather than the first card.

Existing customers first, essentially always.
