This is the uncomfortable finding in legal marketing. Firms buy the most expensive clicks in advertising and then route the resulting calls to a receptionist handling three other things, or to voicemail after five o'clock, when the person calling has just been in a car accident and will call the next firm on the list within the hour.

Published guidance on speed varies by practice area but points the same direction: under five minutes for personal injury and criminal defense, where the decision is urgent and emotional, and under an hour for estate planning, family and business matters. Beyond those windows, response rates fall off sharply.

The layer most agencies skip

Your marketing copy is regulated, and the grievance has your name on it

Every state regulates attorney advertising through its own version of ABA Model Rules 7.1 through 7.3. A marketing agency that writes non-compliant copy faces no consequence. You face the bar complaint.

That asymmetry is the single most important thing to understand about hiring anyone to market a law firm. Vendor conduct can become your grievance, which includes what a lead generator's intake script says to a prospect on your behalf.

Rule:

Nothing on this page is legal advice, and it cannot be. The ABA Model Rules have no force until a state adopts them, states adopt them unevenly, and your own bar's version governs you. What we can do is write marketing that is built to survive that review, flag the copy we think will not, and refuse to publish claims we believe cross a line. The final compliance call is yours and your bar's.

The state-by-state gap is wider than most firms expect

Florida operates one of the most detailed regimes in the country, with specific disclaimer formatting requirements, a rule that many advertisements be filed with the Bar for review before they run, and mandated language that past results do not guarantee future outcomes.

New York requires the words "Attorney Advertising" on a firm's home page, holds firms to multi-year retention of their advertisements, and has told lawyers they may not use "specialist" or "expertise" without accredited certification.

If you practice across state lines, you are subject to the rules of each jurisdiction where you advertise, and the strictest one effectively sets your standard for shared creative. That is a planning constraint, not an afterthought.

Reviews now carry a second layer

Since late 2024 the federal government regulates consumer reviews directly, on top of whatever your bar requires. That means fake reviews, undisclosed incentives for reviews, and testimonials that misrepresent typical outcomes carry exposure beyond the professional conduct rules.

A genuine client saying the team kept them informed is fine in most jurisdictions. A testimonial edited to read as a promised verdict is a problem under both regimes at once.

Paid leads and the fee-sharing line

Lawyers may generally pay the reasonable costs of generating leads. What a lead generator may not do is recommend or vouch for you, and in most states non-lawyers cannot own a share of a law firm or its fees under Rule 5.4. That makes the structure of a pay-per-case arrangement worth reading carefully rather than assuming.

On solicitation, the line runs roughly where initiation does. A prospect who requested information is permissible. Cold outreach to identified accident victims is not. Many states also require disclosure that a lead was paid for.

A routine signed auto case is commonly worth five figures in contingency fees. The auction knows that, which is why legal clicks are priced the way they are. Nothing about this market is irrational, it is just expensive.

Where a small firm can actually win

Not in the broad auction. Large firms dominate generic injury search and broadcast, and outspending them is not a strategy available to a three-attorney practice.

The positions that work are narrower. A specific niche within a practice area, where the auction is thin because the volume is low. Hyper-local search and a genuinely maintained Google Business Profile. Referral relationships with professionals who see your clients before you do. And exclusive leads with tight geographic and practice-area filters rather than shared ones.

All four share a property: they are places where being smaller is not a disadvantage. That is the whole game for a firm that cannot win on budget.

Do this before you spend more

Six ways to fix intake that do not require hiring anyone

If the calculator told you intake is your largest gain, here is what that actually looks like in practice. Most of these are a decision rather than a budget line, and you can act on all six without us.

1. Find out what your real answer rate is

Not what anyone believes it is. Put call tracking on every advertised number, review a month of records, and count what went to voicemail, rang out, or was answered by somebody who took a message. Nearly every firm we do this with is surprised, and the surprise runs in one direction.

2. Decide who owns the phone during hearings

The predictable failure is that everyone is in court at once and the calls route to a voicemail nobody clears until evening. That is a scheduling decision, not a staffing one, and naming a person for each block of the day fixes most of it.

3. Cover the hours your clients actually have problems

Injury and criminal matters do not arrive between nine and five. Evenings and weekends are when people have accidents and arrests, and a firm reachable then competes against firms that are not. An answering service that captures details and books a consultation beats voicemail by a wide margin, and an AI intake assistant costs less than either.

4. Call web form submissions back immediately

A form filled at eleven at night and answered at ten the next morning is usually a client who has already retained someone else. Published guidance points to under five minutes for urgent practice areas. Even a text acknowledging receipt and offering a time holds the position far better than silence.

5. Write down what qualifies a matter

If whoever answers the phone cannot tell a viable case from a non-viable one, they will either waste attorney time on both or turn away good matters on instinct. A one-page qualification script is unglamorous and typically moves the qualification rate more than any campaign change.

6. Follow up more than once

A prospect who did not answer your callback is not a lost case, they are a case you tried to reach once. Three attempts across two days, with at least one outside business hours, recovers a meaningful share of what most firms write off after the first voicemail.

Why give this away. Because you can do all six without an agency, several will pay for themselves inside a month, and a firm that fixes intake first gets far more from any campaign we later run. If these six are already handled and your cost per signed case is still wrong, then the problem genuinely is the marketing, and that is a conversation worth having.
