    Four ways to put software in someone's hand

    They are not interchangeable, and the gap between the cheapest and the most expensive is roughly an order of magnitude. Most of the value an app development company adds happens in this decision, before anyone writes code.

    Native appWritten twice, in Swift and Kotlin

    Fastest, smoothest, and the only option when you need deep hardware access such as background location, tight camera control or heavy on-device processing. You maintain two separate codebases forever, which is exactly as expensive as it sounds.

    Highest costTwo builds, two release cycles, two sets of bugs
    Cross-platform appReact Native or Flutter

    One codebase ships to both stores. For the overwhelming majority of business apps the user cannot tell the difference, and published industry guidance puts the saving at 30–50% against building two native apps.

    30–50% less than nativeThe sensible default for most businesses
    Progressive web appInstalls from a browser

    Adds to the home screen, sends push notifications, works offline for cached content, and skips app review entirely. No store listing, no 15–30% commission on digital sales, and updates go live the moment you publish them.

    No store, no commissionLoses background location and heavy hardware access
    Web appRuns in any browser, on any device

    Dashboards, booking systems, quoting tools, portals, internal software. If people reach it from a laptop as often as a phone, this is almost always the right answer. We cover this in depth on the web app development page.

    Lowest cost to launchCheapest to change once it is live

    There is a fifth option nobody sells you, and it is often correct: fix the mobile experience of the website you already have. A page that loads fast and converts well beats an app that 95% of downloaders abandon inside a month. That is the entire argument of our work as a conversion rate optimization agency, and it applies to software as much as to landing pages.

    Downloads are not the finish line

    An app that keeps 5% of its installs after a month needs twenty downloads to hold onto one user. If your plan is to promote the app to your existing customers, you are paying app prices to reach people who already know you.

    Retention has one reliable lever, and it is not features. Apps that get a person to a genuinely useful moment inside the first session hold two to three times more of them a week later. That is a conversion problem wearing a software costume, which is why we scope the first screen before we scope the feature list.

    And then there is review

    Apple reviewed 7,771,599 submissions in 2024 and rejected 1,931,400 of them. In 2025 it reviewed more than 9.1 million and rejected over two million. Google Play is far more permissive on the way in, at roughly 8% rejected in 2025, but removed over two million apps after publication that same year.

    Apple's most-cited categories are performance, legal, design and business, in that order. The one that catches small businesses is guideline 4.2, minimum functionality — if your app is essentially your website in a wrapper, it does not get in. That single rule invalidates a large share of the app ideas we are asked to quote.

    Launch extras cover store compliance, transaction fees, legal and privacy work, and the marketing needed to get anyone to install the thing. GoodFirms puts them at 25–35% of project cost, and they are the line that surprises people. Maintenance at 15–20% of build cost per year is not optional either — operating systems change twice a year, and an app that stops working on the current iOS is an app that gets deleted.

    How the build actually runs

    Six stages, in this order, because each one kills work that the next one would have wasted. The first two happen before you commit to a build budget.

    Decide whether it should exist

    We map who uses it, how often, and what job it does that your website cannot. If a progressive web app or a better mobile site does the job, we say so here and the conversation gets much cheaper. This is also where we check your idea against Apple's minimum functionality rule, before you find out the expensive way.

    Define the smallest version that works

    One core workflow, done properly, shipped early. Every feature that survives this cut has to earn its place against the hours it adds. The list of things we deliberately left out gets written down too, so nobody re-argues it in month four.

    Design the first sixty seconds

    Retention is decided in the first session. We design the path from cold open to a genuinely useful moment before we design anything else, because everything past that point is worthless if people never arrive.

    Build in visible increments

    You get something installable on your own phone early and often. Two-week increments, working software each time, no six-month reveal. Cross-platform unless there is a hardware reason not to be.

    Test on the phones people actually own

    Crashes and bugs are the single largest rejection category, and most of them show up on mid-range Android hardware that never gets tested because the team all carry flagships. We test on real devices, not just simulators, and we prepare the review submission properly the first time.

    Watch what people do, then fix it

    Install to first-use, first-use to return, return to habit. Those three numbers tell you what to build next far better than a feature request ever will. This is the same discipline we apply to conversion optimization, pointed at software instead of pages.

    Be honest about which column you are in

    We would rather lose the build than take money for something we would advise a friend against. Here is how the two sides usually look.

    An app earns its keep when

    • People open it weekly or daily as part of doing their job or running their life
    • It needs the camera, background location, offline data or hardware a browser cannot reach
    • Your field crews, drivers or technicians work where signal drops out
    • You are replacing paper, radio calls or a spreadsheet that costs real hours every week
    • The app is the product and customers pay to use it
    • Push notifications drive a behavior that makes you money, not just noise

    You want something else when

    • Customers would use it a few times a year, like booking a service or checking an order
    • It would show the same content your website already shows
    • The real goal is looking modern, and an app is the shape that took
    • You have no plan or budget to market the app after launch
    • Nobody internally owns it after the agency leaves
    • The problem is that your website converts badly, which an app will not fix

    That second column is not a soft no. It is usually a redirect to a web app, a progressive web app, or a rebuilt mobile site that costs a fraction as much and gets used more. We build all of those too, so the recommendation costs us nothing to make honestly.

    App development questions we get asked

    An app development company scopes, designs, builds, tests and launches software that runs on phones and tablets, then maintains it as operating systems change. In practice the highest-value part of the job happens before any code is written: deciding whether the idea needs a native app, a cross-platform app, a progressive web app or a plain web app, because that single decision changes the cost by roughly an order of magnitude. Search Converts also runs the marketing channels that would promote the app, so the launch plan is built alongside the product rather than handed over afterward.

    Published 2026 guidance puts a focused first version at roughly $15,000 to $50,000, a full business app at $40,000 to $80,000, and a complex platform at $80,000 to $250,000 or more. Those figures assume a cross-platform build with a professional team. Building two separate native apps raises it substantially, and offshore teams reduce it by 40 to 70% with a corresponding increase in management effort. You need a web app or progressive web app when people use it occasionally, reach it from laptops as well as phones, or when the content is the same thing your website already shows.
