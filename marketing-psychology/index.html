Marketing psychology · color · pricing · proof

People don't read your website. They feel it first.

Then they look for a reason to trust the feeling. Give them one.

Direct answer: Marketing psychology describes the well-supported patterns in how people decide: attention follows contrast and hierarchy rather than a color's supposed meaning; the first number seen anchors judgments of price; a middle "decoy" option shifts choice toward it; proof placed beside an offer reduces perceived risk; and a single clear call to action outperforms several competing ones. Search Converts applies these in conversion optimization and publishes them here with sources, avoiding pop-psychology claims.

Color, honestly

Color does three jobs. "Meaning" isn't one of them.

Red doesn't make people buy and blue doesn't make people trust — not reliably, not across cultures, not across contexts. What color reliably does: it gets attention when it contrasts, it tells the eye where to go when it's used consistently, and it makes one action obvious when only one thing is that color. That's why this site has one red button and everything else is quiet.

Attention follows contrast

The button people click is the one that stands out from its surroundings — whatever hue it is. Test it above: the "winning" color changes with the background.

Hierarchy follows consistency

The same color for the same action, every page. The eye learns it in two screens. Change it and the visitor has to relearn your site.

One button per screen

Five good options produce fewer decisions than one clear one. Choice overload is one of the better-replicated findings in the field. Cut to the action that pays.

Price, proof and the decoy

The first number sets the frame. The middle option wins.

Anchoring: people judge a price against the first number they saw. Show the cost of the problem before the cost of the fix, and the fix looks reasonable. The decoy effect: three options with a deliberately unattractive middle push choice toward the one you want — it's why "good / better / best" exists. Psychological pricing: $99 reads meaningfully cheaper than $100 because we read left to right; use it honestly, on real prices.

Proof beside the ask: a review next to the button reduces perceived risk at the moment of decision; the same review on an "About" page does almost nothing. Loss framing: "what waiting costs" moves more people than "what you could gain," which is why our tools show it. Reciprocity: give something real first — a free audit, a working tool — and people are more willing to give back. None of this is manipulation when the offer is honest; it's just not making people work to say yes.

Questions

Straight answers.

Contrast, consistency and hierarchy work reliably. Specific color 'meanings' (red = urgency, blue = trust) are weak, culture-dependent and easily overridden by context. Design for contrast and one clear action, then test.

Prices set to influence perception — $99 instead of $100, anchoring against a higher first number, a decoy middle option. Used on honest prices, it's simply presenting value clearly; used to trick people, it costs you the customer's second purchase.

Adding a third, deliberately less attractive option shifts choice toward the option next to it. It's why three-tier pricing is everywhere. We use it only when the middle option is genuinely the right one for most buyers.

Next to the decision: a review or credential within one screen of the button. Proof on a separate page reaches almost no one at the moment they need it.

It's manipulation if the offer is dishonest. If the offer is real, this is just not making people work to say yes. We won't use fake urgency, invented reviews or countdown timers — they win a sale and lose a customer.

Start with the anchoring and choice-overload literature in behavioral economics (Kahneman and Tversky's work on anchoring; Iyengar and Lepper on choice), and the persuasion principles summarized by Cialdini. We'll send a reading list if you ask.
