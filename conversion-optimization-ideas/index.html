53 conversion ideas, sorted by whether you can actually test them

Every list like this assumes you can run an A/B test. Most sites cannot: the median test needs somewhere between 10,400 and 14,800 visitors per variant, and only around 9% of sites see 100,000 visitors a month. So this list marks each idea as something to test or something to just fix, and gives you a calculator to work out which camp you are in.

Most conversion advice is written as though testing is free

It is not. Testing costs traffic, time and the discipline to leave a test alone once it is running, and the published data on how that usually goes is worth seeing before you plan a program around it.

Win rates are lower than anyone advertises

Optimizely's analysis of more than 127,000 experiments puts the average win rate near 12%, meaning roughly one tested idea in eight produces a statistically significant improvement. Platform-published benchmarks cluster in the same region: VWO around 14%, CXL and Convert around 20%. An independent audit of 2,288 tests found half beat their control on raw numbers but only 19.1% did so with statistical significance.

Mature programs with strong hypothesis generation reach 22 to 30%. That is the ceiling for disciplined teams, not the starting point.

The traffic requirement is the real barrier

Across a dataset of 4,200 tests, the median required sample was 10,400 visitors per variant server-side and 14,800 client-side. Meanwhile only around 9% of sites reach 100,000 monthly visitors, and roughly 0.2% of websites run testing tools at all.

Put those together and the conclusion is uncomfortable but useful: for most businesses, A/B testing is not the right tool. That does not mean conversion work is unavailable to you. It means the work is fixing known problems rather than testing unknowns, which is what the tags on the list below are for.

Lifts are smaller than the case studies suggest

The median lift from a winning test is around 6.1%. Fifty percent improvements sit at the 95th to 99th percentile of outcomes, which is to say they happen and they are not a plan. The value comes from compounding: a 6.1% median lift across eight to ten winning tests a year on a checkout funnel produces something in the region of 47 to 61% total.

And most tests are stopped too early

Tests run under 14 days carry a false positive rate around 61%, and 41.4% of tests in one large dataset claimed significance while under-powered. Checking daily and stopping when the variant looks good is reportedly done by 47% of teams and is the single most common way to generate a fake winner. Underpowered tests also exaggerate the lift they do find, which is why implemented winners so often fail to reproduce their gains.

The honest summary. If you have the traffic, test, and expect to be wrong seven times out of eight. If you do not have the traffic, stop trying to test, fix the things that are already known to be broken, and measure at the business level rather than the page level. Both are legitimate. Pretending you are doing the first when you are actually doing the second is what wastes years.

Sources: Optimizely analysis of 127,000+ experiments; independent audit of 2,288 tests reporting raw and statistically significant win rates alongside platform benchmarks; A/B test program data covering 4,200+ experiments for sample size, power and lift distribution; agency experiment database of 90+ ecommerce brands for win rate by funnel stage and median duration; published CRO statistics roundups for testing tool adoption and site traffic distribution. Figures are drawn largely from ecommerce and should be read as directional.

A rule that saves a lot of wasted months. If a test would take more than about six weeks, do not run it. Seasonality, traffic mix and your own site changes will contaminate the result long before it finishes, and you will have spent six weeks learning something unreliable about one button.

Two things this makes visible immediately. First, low baseline conversion rates are punishing: halving your conversion rate roughly doubles the traffic you need. Second, chasing small lifts is where testing programs go to die, because detecting a 5% improvement costs roughly four times the traffic of detecting a 10% one.

It is a signal to stop testing and start fixing, which is what most of the list below is for. Once you have made the known fixes and traffic has grown, testing becomes available again.

If you cannot test

How to know your fixes worked when A/B testing is off the table

This is the part nobody writes about, because it is less impressive than experimentation. It is also the situation most businesses are actually in.

Measure at the business level, not the page level

You cannot detect a 6% page-level lift without a lot of traffic. You can absolutely detect whether inquiries went from eleven a month to nineteen. Lower-traffic sites usually have larger, chunkier problems, and fixing them produces changes visible in the monthly total rather than requiring a significance calculation.

Use before-and-after windows honestly

Compare equal periods, account for seasonality, and note every other thing that changed in the window. This is weaker evidence than a controlled test and it is not worthless. The failure mode is claiming causation for a change that coincided with your busy season, so write down what else happened.

Batch your changes deliberately

If you cannot isolate variables anyway, stop pretending. Ship a group of related fixes at once, measure the total effect, and accept that you will not know which one did the work. That is a reasonable trade when the alternative is shipping nothing for eight months.

Watch the qualitative signals

Session recordings, the questions your sales team keeps answering, support tickets about the same confusion, and the point in a form where people stop. These do not prove anything statistically and they routinely find the actual problem faster than a test would, because they tell you what is happening rather than only that something is.

Run a holdout where the mechanism allows

Email flows, direct mail drops and broadcast campaigns can all be measured by withholding a random group, and that works at far lower volumes than page testing. Anywhere you control who receives something, you have a cleaner measurement available than anywhere you only control what a page looks like.
