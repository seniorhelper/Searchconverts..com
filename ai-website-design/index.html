What actually happened

The year AI coding went mainstream, the web got worse

WebAIM has scanned the home pages of the top one million websites every year since 2019. For six consecutive years the numbers improved. The 2026 report ended that run.

Across those million pages, automated testing detected 56,114,377 distinct accessibility errors, an average of 56.1 per page and a 10.1% rise on the previous year. The share of pages with detectable WCAG failures rose from 94.8% to 95.9%. Because automated tools catch only a portion of real barriers, the true figure is higher.

WebAIM's own report names likely causes, and this is the part worth reading twice. It points to increased reliance on third-party frameworks and libraries, and to automated or AI-assisted coding practices, the thing people have started calling vibe coding.

The same six failures, seven years running

These six categories account for 96% of every error detected. Four of the six got worse in 2026. Low contrast, the most widespread, jumped from 79.1% to 83.9% of home pages.

Home pages are getting heavier, fast

The average home page now contains 1,437 elements, a 22.5% increase in one year and close to double what it was seven years ago. More elements means more surface area for things to break.

Why this belongs on a page selling AI website design. Because we use these tools daily and they are genuinely excellent at production. What they are not is accountable. A model generates the pattern it has seen most, and the pattern it has seen most is a page with 56 accessibility errors on it. Using AI well means knowing what to check afterward, and that is a human job for the foreseeable future.
The demonstration

A default and a decision, side by side

Both of these are competent. One is what you get when nobody made a choice, and you have seen it four hundred times this year. Switch between them and watch what actually changes.

Generated default versus designed page

Same business, same content, same amount of work to build. Entirely different result.

Everything is centered

Center alignment is the safest choice and therefore the most common one. It gives every element equal weight, which means nothing leads.

Three cards, always three

Equal boxes with equal icons imply your three services matter equally. They almost never do, and the page never tells anyone which to pick.

Grey on grey

Low contrast is the single most common accessibility failure on the web, found on 83.9% of home pages, and it is what default palettes produce.

Nothing about the first version is incompetent. That is exactly the problem. It is a page that could belong to any company in any industry, which means it does no work at all for yours.

The second version makes four decisions the first one avoided. One thing is biggest, so the eye knows where to start. The layout is asymmetric, so it reads in an order rather than all at once. One call to action is dominant instead of two competing at equal weight. And the contrast is high enough to actually read, which is a design choice before it is a compliance one.

An AI tool will happily produce either. It defaults to the first because the first is what the training data is full of, and defaults are what you get when nobody makes a decision.

The second meaning

AI website design also means designing for AI

Increasingly your page has two audiences: the person reading it and the model deciding whether to cite it in an answer. Both want the same thing, which is a page whose structure actually describes its content.

This is the useful convergence in modern web work. Semantic structure, real headings in a sensible order, labeled form fields, described images, and text that is text rather than a picture of text. Screen readers need it. Answer engines need it. Search has always rewarded it.

Being specific

Where we use AI, and where we refuse to

Most agencies selling AI website design are vague about this on purpose. Here is the actual split, because you are entitled to know what a machine wrote and what a person decided.

Where it genuinely helps

Scaffolding components, converting a design into working markup, writing repetitive variants, generating alt text drafts for a hundred images, catching inconsistencies across templates, and producing structured data. These are the tasks where speed matters and there is a correct answer to check against.

It is also excellent at first drafts of things nobody enjoys writing, like the fourteenth service page. The draft still gets rewritten, but starting from something beats starting from nothing.

Where it actively hurts

Positioning, hierarchy and voice. A model produces the median of what it has seen, and the median is precisely what you are trying not to be. Ask it what makes your business different and it will confidently tell you about quality, service and trust, which is what it told the last four thousand people who asked.

It also cannot tell you what to leave out, which is most of design. And it will generate confident, plausible, wrong claims about your business that you will not notice until a customer does.

Where it must be checked by a person

Everything that ships. Contrast ratios, keyboard navigation, focus order, heading hierarchy, form labels, and whether the generated markup is semantic or a pile of nested containers with styling attached. The 2026 data is fairly direct about what happens when this step is skipped at scale.

That is the whole method, and it is not complicated. Use the tool for production, use a person for judgment, and test the result rather than trusting it.

Design and build

Custom pages built to load fast and read clearly, from a real design decision rather than a template with your logo dropped into it.

Accessibility as standard

Contrast, keyboard paths, labels, landmarks and heading order checked before launch, not sold back to you later as a remediation project.

Schema, semantic markup and clean content structure so answer engines can quote you accurately. The wider strategy sits with AI marketing.

Do this yourself

Six checks you can run on your own site in twenty minutes

None of these need a tool subscription or a developer. They catch the majority of what actually goes wrong, and you can do all six before you speak to anyone, including us.

1. Unplug your mouse

Press Tab repeatedly and try to reach every link, button and form field on your home page. Watch for a visible outline showing where you are. If focus disappears, jumps somewhere illogical, or gets trapped inside a menu you cannot escape, keyboard users cannot use your site. This is a pass or fail, not a percentage.

2. Read it in bright sunlight

Take your phone outside. Any text you have to squint at is failing contrast, and low contrast is the most common accessibility failure on the web at 83.9% of home pages. Light grey body text on white is the usual culprit, and it is almost always a decision someone made because it looked elegant on a good monitor in a dark room.

3. Try to select the text

Drag across your headline and key selling points. If nothing highlights, that text is an image. Search engines cannot read it, screen readers cannot announce it, answer engines cannot quote it, and it will not resize for anyone who needs larger type.

4. Ask an AI assistant what you do

Paste your web address into any AI assistant and ask what the business does, who it serves and where. If the answer is vague, wrong, or describes a generic agency, that is roughly what any answer engine will produce when someone asks about your category. The fix is usually clearer content and structured data rather than more content.

5. Count the things asking for attention

Look at your home page and count the elements competing to be clicked first. If there are more than two, nothing is primary. The most common finding when we audit a page is not that the call to action is weak, but that there are five of them at equal weight.

6. Load it on a bad connection

Turn off wifi, drop to a weak signal, and load your slowest page. Under half of mobile sites currently deliver good Core Web Vitals, and the median mobile home page has been getting heavier rather than lighter. If you are watching a blank screen while a font or a script decides whether to arrive, so is everyone else.

Why we publish this. Because you can act on all six without us, and several will fix real problems for free. If you run them and everything passes, you probably do not need a new website, which is worth knowing before anyone quotes you for one. If three of them fail, you now know exactly what to ask an agency about, and you can tell whether the answer is any good.
