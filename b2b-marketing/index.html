Your pipeline report covers about 5% of your market

Only a twentieth of B2B buyers are in market in any given quarter. The rest will buy eventually, from whoever they already recognize by then. Most B2B marketing budgets are built entirely around the twentieth.

How many accounts are actually buying right now?

Set your market size and how often companies in your category replace what you sell. The arithmetic does the rest, and it is usually sobering.

The 95:5 market model

Based on the Ehrenberg-Bass finding that in-market share is roughly the inverse of your category's purchase cycle.

The rule comes from Professor John Dawes at the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, published in 2021 and carried into mainstream B2B thinking by LinkedIn's B2B Institute. It is a heuristic rather than a law. Intent data vendors argue the in-market share is higher than 5%, and for some categories it is.

The shape holds either way. Most of the companies who will buy from you in the next four years are not buying today, and nothing in your pipeline report knows they exist.

The part that makes this expensive. Ehrenberg-Bass surveys found the vast majority of B2B marketers expect significant sales within the first two weeks of a campaign. If only a twentieth of the market is buying this quarter, and your category takes months to close, then a two-week judgment window guarantees you will cancel the work that reaches future buyers and keep only the work that harvests the ones already deciding. That is how a company ends up with an efficient pipeline and a shrinking market.
What your CRM cannot see

The deal starts long before the first conversation

Forrester benchmarks put enterprise technology purchases at 11 to 17 months from first touch to closed won. The portion your sales team witnesses is typically the last three to five months of that.

One enterprise deal, drawn to scale

Everything to the left of the marker happened before anyone in your company knew the account existed.

Forrester 2024 benchmarks for enterprise technology purchases, with 6sense analysis of anonymous buying behavior indicating the visible portion is typically the final three to five months. Demand Gen Report found more than half of B2B buyers spend three months or more in active research before engaging a sales representative.

Two consequences follow, and neither is comfortable.

The first is that last-click attribution is structurally incapable of describing this. Whatever touch happened closest to the form fill gets the credit, and the eleven months of category education that made the account consider you at all gets nothing. Cut the budget the model says is unproductive and you will feel fine for two quarters.

The second is that most of the decision is made where you are not present. Gartner reports buyers spend only 17% of total purchase time meeting with potential suppliers, and split across three or four vendors that leaves any single sales team with roughly 5 to 6% of the buyer's attention. Your rep is not driving the process. Your rep is being briefly consulted during it.

What that argues for is fairly specific: content that answers the questions being asked while you are absent, sales enablement that survives being forwarded to someone you never met, and measurement that includes asking buyers directly how they found you rather than trusting a model that cannot see eleven months of the story.

Who you are actually selling to

There is no decision maker. There is a committee, and it is arguing.

Gartner's 2024 B2B Buying Survey puts complex purchases at six to ten decision makers, each arriving with four or five pieces of information they gathered independently. Forrester puts the average enterprise buying group at 13 people.

Do the arithmetic on that. A committee of eight, each holding five independently sourced facts, is forty pieces of information in the room, most of which you did not supply and cannot see. Your carefully written pitch deck is one input among many, and several of the others are competitor comparisons, peer opinions and something a colleague read last year.

Consensus is the product you are actually selling

Gartner reported in 2025 that 74% of buying teams experience unhealthy conflict, and that committees reaching genuine consensus are 2.5 times more likely to describe the outcome as a high-quality decision. Forrester's State of Business Buying found 86% of B2B purchases stall somewhere in the process, and 81% of buyers end up dissatisfied with the provider they chose.

Read those together and the job changes. You are not persuading a person. You are handing a champion the material they need to win an internal argument you will never attend. That is why the most valuable asset in most B2B marketing is not a landing page. It is a one-page business case your champion can forward to a CFO without editing it.

It also explains why 77% of buyers describe their most recent purchase as complex or difficult, and why 75% say they would prefer a rep-free experience for as long as possible. The friction is not your sales team. The friction is the committee, and most vendor content makes it worse by talking about the product instead of the decision.

Gartner's six buying jobs. Buyers loop through them repeatedly rather than moving in order, which is why a linear funnel misdescribes what is happening. It is common for a committee to reopen requirements after a vendor believed that stage was finished.

The finding that reorders your budget

Most buyers arrive with a vendor already in mind

Forrester found 92% of B2B buyers begin their journey with at least one vendor already in mind, and 41% start with a single preferred vendor.

That is the whole argument for brand work in a single statistic, and it is not a soft one.

If nine in ten buying processes open with a shortlist that already exists, then the competition for a place on that list happened months or years earlier, while nobody was in market and nothing was measurable. By the time your beautifully optimized bottom-funnel campaign reaches them, the list is written and you are either on it or you are the third quote they collect for procurement.

This is what the 95:5 rule is actually for. The point is not that brand advertising is nice. The point is that mental availability at the moment a company enters the market is decided beforehand, and the only way to be on that list is to have been recognizable during the long stretch when nothing was happening.

None of which means abandoning demand capture. Someone is searching for your category today and they should find you. It means the budget needs two jobs in it, and only one of them will look good in a monthly report.

Reporting

How to measure this without lying to yourself

If the cycle runs eleven months and 80% of it is invisible, then any attribution model that assigns credit to a single touch is producing fiction with a confidence interval.

The failure mode is predictable. Last-click credits the branded search that happened after the buyer already knew your name. The channel that created the recognition looks unproductive. Budget moves toward capture, awareness decays quietly, and eighteen months later the pipeline thins for reasons the dashboard cannot explain.

What we do instead is unglamorous and works better.

Ask buyers directly

A single open field on the form asking how they heard about you, plus the same question on discovery calls, consistently produces a different and more honest picture than the tracking does. It is not precise. It is far less wrong than a model that cannot see eleven months.

Measure the market, not just the pipeline

Track share of the accounts you target that recognize you, how many arrive already knowing your name, and whether the proportion of inbound deals that mention you unprompted is rising. Those are leading indicators for a cycle this long. Pipeline is a lagging one.

Judge by cohort, not by month

If the cycle is eleven months, comparing this month's spend to this month's closed revenue compares two unrelated things. Group deals by when the first touch happened and read the cohort as it matures. Slower to report, and it answers the actual question.

Count qualified opportunities, not MQLs

A marketing qualified lead is a measure of form fills, and form fills are trivially easy to manufacture. Opportunity creation, win rate by source and revenue per account are harder to fake and closer to the thing you care about. The same discipline we apply as a conversion rate optimization agency: count the outcome at the end of the chain, because everything before it is only worth what survives.

The engagement

What a B2B marketing engagement covers here

Two jobs running at once: reach the 95% who will buy later, and convert the 5% who are deciding now. Most agencies sell only the second and quietly blame the market when growth stalls.

Demand capture

Search, retargeting and review-site presence aimed at the accounts already evaluating. Highest intent, smallest audience, fastest feedback. Detail on PPC management.

Demand creation

Reaching the out-of-market majority so you are recognized when they enter. Video, connected TV, programmatic and social used for memory rather than for clicks. See programmatic advertising.

Content for the six buying jobs

Buying guides, requirements templates, honest comparisons and business case material built for the committee rather than for a keyword. This is the highest-leverage content in B2B and almost nobody writes it.

Account-based programs

Named account targeting where the deal size justifies it, coordinated across roles rather than aimed at a single champion. Underpinned by lead generation built for close rate.

Long-cycle nurture

Sequences that stay useful across eleven months without pestering, so you are present when the committee reopens requirements. See email marketing.

Sales enablement that survives forwarding

Material your champion can send to a CFO without editing it, plus coaching on the 5% of buyer time your team actually gets. Runs alongside sales training.

Questions we get

B2B marketing questions, answered with the research

A B2B marketing agency helps a business win customers whose purchases involve multiple decision makers, formal evaluation and long timelines. In practice that means two jobs running at once: capturing demand from the small share of accounts evaluating now, and creating familiarity among the much larger share who will buy later. It also means producing material for a buying committee rather than a single buyer, and measuring in a way that survives a cycle where most of the decision happens before your sales team is involved.

It is the finding, published by Professor John Dawes of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute in 2021 and popularized by LinkedIn's B2B Institute, that only about 5% of business buyers are in market to purchase in any given quarter. The remaining 95% will buy eventually, often years later. Because in-market share is roughly the inverse of the category purchase cycle, a category where companies buy every five years has about 20% in market per year and 5% per quarter. It is a heuristic rather than a law, and intent data vendors argue the true share is higher in some categories, but the implication holds: marketing aimed only at active buyers ignores most of your future customers.

Both, and the split depends on your category cycle. If companies in your market buy every five years, only about 5% are in market this quarter, so a budget spent entirely on capture is competing for a twentieth of the opportunity while ignoring the accounts that will buy in years two through five. Equally, abandoning capture to build brand means losing the deals happening right now. The practical answer is to fund both and, crucially, to measure them differently, because judging brand work on this month's pipeline guarantees you will cancel it.
