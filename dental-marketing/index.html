Why the published averages disagree

Case acceptance is not one number

Search for a benchmark and you will find figures from 25% to 70%, all presented as the industry average. They are not contradicting each other. They are measuring different mixes of treatment.

Preventive and hygiene recommendations are accepted at 85 to 95%, because they are cheap, familiar and usually covered. Crowns, implants and cosmetic work land between 50 and 65% in most general practices. Blend those together in different proportions and you can generate almost any average you like, which is exactly what has happened to the published figures.

So a single blended acceptance rate tells you almost nothing about where your practice is losing money. Tracked by procedure category, it tells you everything.

Acceptance ranges by procedure category from 2026 dental practice benchmarking. Anything under 50% on restorative treatment is generally read as a system problem in how care is presented rather than a patient problem, which is a useful distinction because one of those is fixable this quarter.

The practical consequence for marketing is uncomfortable. A practice at 46% blended acceptance that doubles its new patient flow will roughly double the value of the treatment it fails to schedule. You get busier, your unscheduled treatment report gets longer, and the production line moves less than the ad invoice suggests it should.

That is why we ask about case acceptance on the first call. It is not scope creep. It decides whether the marketing we sell you will actually work.

Context

Why 2026 feels harder than it should

If the practice feels busier and less profitable at the same time, the market data agrees with you.

The ADA's Health Policy Institute reported in the first quarter of 2026 that roughly one third of dentists were not busy enough. That is a demand signal, and it is the reason more practices are advertising than were three years ago.

At the same time, consumer dental spending grew about 4% over the prior twelve months while reimbursement rates failed to keep pace with inflation or practice costs. More than 40% of dentists now name overhead as their leading challenge. So the money coming in grew a little, the cost of earning it grew more, and the gap has to come from somewhere.

The average general dentist produced $942,290 in gross billings in 2024. Average daily gross production sat near $9,991 while the top decile of offices reached $17,874, which is close to double from the same number of chairs and the same number of hours. That spread is not explained by marketing budget. It is explained by what happens to a patient once they are in the building.

The uncomfortable read for an agency to publish is that in a market like this, advertising is the second lever, not the first. It works, and we sell it, but it works considerably better on a practice that already schedules what it diagnoses.

Sources: American Dental Association Health Policy Institute, State of the US Dental Economy, first quarter 2026; Henry Schein One industry production reporting and the 2026 Catalyst Index benchmarking report; 2025 Dental Economics and Levin Group practice survey.

Before the chair

The two failures that happen before anyone sits down

The phone

A dental practice is a phone business with clinical equipment attached. An analysis of more than a million connected patient calls published in 2026 found that only 64.63% of patient calls actually reached a staff member, and of the opportunities that were missed, fewer than 26% received any follow-up attempt at all.

That means the most expensive thing in a dental marketing budget is often the ring tone. A caller who reaches voicemail at 11:40am does not wait. They call the practice on the next line of the search results, and your advertising has just funded a competitor's new patient. We cover the underlying call data in more depth on our healthcare marketing page, because the pattern holds across every provider type.

The fix is unglamorous and cheap relative to media: coverage during lunch and after hours, a documented callback rule with a time limit attached, and someone accountable for the missed call log each morning.

The broken appointment

Industry average sits around 7.4%, and well-run practices hold under 5%. On a schedule producing $10,000 a day, the difference between 7.4% and 5% is roughly a day of production a month walking out of the calendar. Multi-touch reminders, self-service rescheduling and a genuine short-notice list to backfill gaps move it further than a cancellation fee does, and without the relationship cost.

Both of these sit upstream of everything an agency normally sells. That is precisely why we look at them first, and why the recommendation on a first call is sometimes to spend less on advertising for a quarter and fix the front desk instead.

The engagement

What we do for a dental practice

Roughly in this order. The first three cost far less than the last three and usually produce more, which is an awkward thing for an agency to admit and the reason we lead with it.

Work the unscheduled treatment

A systematic pass through diagnosed treatment that never got scheduled, with follow-up inside 48 hours of the plan being presented rather than a quarterly report nobody reads. This is the fastest production available in most practices.

Case presentation and financing

Visual presentation chairside, the clinical conversation separated from the financial one, and monthly payment options on the table before the patient has to ask. Presentation is a coachable skill, which is why it sits alongside our sales training work.

Recall and reactivation

Practices with structured recall systems retain 85% or more of active patients annually against 60 to 70% without one. Reactivating a lapsed patient costs a fraction of buying a new one. Built with marketing automation.

Answer the phone, then book the call

Coverage, missed-call recovery and scripting for the conversation that turns an inquiry into an appointment. Measured on booked, kept visits rather than call volume.

Local search advertising

Procedure and emergency searches inside your actual catchment, with landing pages that answer the question asked and a new-patient offer priced against your real acceptance economics. See PPC management.

Booking that works after hours

A meaningful share of dental decisions get made in the evening. Online scheduling that reflects genuine availability captures them. Custom scheduling tools fall under web app development.

Organic local search is its own discipline and we treat it as one. Ranking a practice in the map pack and the local results is slower, more technical and more location-specific than paid acquisition, so that work runs through our search arm rather than being bundled in here. If organic is what you need, start with their dentist SEO work.

Everything on our side is measured against booked and kept appointments and, where you can give us the data, against accepted treatment. That is the same discipline behind our work as a conversion rate optimization agency: count the thing at the end of the chain, because every step before it is only worth what survives.

Honest scoping

When to call, and when not to

Call us if

  • Your unscheduled treatment report is long and nobody owns working it
  • Case acceptance is below 55% and you have never tracked it by procedure type
  • Hygiene reappointment is under 85% and patients leave without the next visit booked
  • Nobody can tell you how many calls went unanswered last week
  • You have chair time to fill and an associate to keep busy
  • You are opening a second location or adding a high-value service line

Do not call us if

  • You are booked six weeks out and more demand would only lengthen the wait
  • You want a guaranteed number of new patients before anyone has seen your numbers
  • Nothing about how the phone is covered is going to change
  • You want the marketing without any conversation about what happens after the patient arrives
  • You are looking purely for organic rankings, which belongs with a search specialist
  • The practice is being sold this year and the goal is a short-term production spike

Questions we get

Dental marketing questions, answered with benchmarks

A dental marketing agency helps a practice attract new patients and get more production from the patients it already has. The visible half is advertising, search, websites and offers. The half that decides whether any of it pays is what happens after contact: whether the phone gets answered, whether the appointment gets booked and kept, whether diagnosed treatment gets scheduled, and whether patients come back for recall. Search Converts works both halves and measures to booked, kept appointments rather than to leads, because a lead that never sits in a chair is an expense.

Benchmarking data from 2026 puts the average practice at about 30 new patients a month, with the top 10% of practices seeing 81. A commonly used per-provider benchmark is around 20 new patients per month per provider. Before chasing the top-decile number, check whether you have the chair time to treat them: top performers usually get there through stronger access and scheduling systems rather than through larger advertising budgets.

Rather than a percentage rule, work backwards from capacity and economics. Establish what a new patient is genuinely worth to you at your acceptance rate, what your chair utilization currently is, and how many patients you could actually treat next month. Then set spend so that cost per acquired patient sits comfortably inside that value. Practices that pick a percentage of collections first tend to either underspend while chairs sit empty or overspend into a schedule that cannot absorb the patients.
