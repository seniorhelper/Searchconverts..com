Plain English

What a video production
company does now

A video production company plans, shoots and edits video for a business. What has changed is the deliverable. Video for business used to mean one polished film a year. Video marketing now needs a steady supply of varied concepts, cut to the specifications of several different placements, produced fast enough to replace what fatigues.

That shift happened because everything else got automated. Targeting is largely automatic. Bidding is automatic. Placement selection is automatic. The one input platforms cannot generate for you is something worth watching.

So creative stopped being decoration and became the actual lever. Analysis of digital campaigns attributes roughly 56% of sales lift to creative quality, ahead of media placement and targeting combined. Meanwhile the budget split in most businesses is close to the reverse.

There is a second number that explains everything else on this page: only 5 to 10% of tested creatives become winners. That ratio is not a sign of bad work. It is the normal shape of the thing. It means the question is not how to make one perfect video — it is how to make enough attempts that a winner shows up.

The uncomfortable part

is often the wrong purchase

Why viewers behave this way

The explanation is behavioral rather than aesthetic. People scroll past production value and stop for relatability. A founder talking plainly about a problem outperforms a cinematic thirty-second spot in a feed, because the feed is a place people go to see other people rather than to watch advertising.

The algorithms compound it. Watch time and engagement velocity drive distribution, and content that looks native to the platform earns both. A beautiful ad that announces itself as an ad in the first half second gets scrolled, and the platform learns from that.

Where polish still earns its money

Not everywhere is a feed. Connected TV puts your work on a large screen in front of somebody sitting down, and poor production is punishing at that size. Older audiences on Facebook respond better to brand signaling. Explainers, product demonstrations and anything establishing credibility for a considered purchase benefit from being made properly.

The answer is therefore not "always go cheap." It is matching the production tier to the placement, which is a boring answer that happens to be correct.

What the numbers do not say

They do not say studio production is worthless. They say that if your objective is finding a winning ad, attempts matter more than any single attempt. Twenty concepts at a modest tier will beat one beautiful film at finding the thing that works, and then you have something worth spending properly on.

That sequencing is the actual recommendation: test cheap, scale what wins, then invest in production behind the proven concept. Doing it in the other order means paying studio rates to discover that the idea did not land.

The line item people forget

Usage rights. A creator producing content for your organic channels and a creator licensing that content for paid advertising are two different transactions at two different prices. Darkposting rights — running an ad that appears to come from the creator's own profile — typically add around 15% of the base rate per month.

Agree this before production rather than after, because renegotiating rights on a video that is already performing is an expensive conversation to have.

How we work

Production as a pipeline,
not a project

  1. Start with the placement, not the script. Where this runs decides aspect ratio, length, whether it can be skipped and how much polish is worth paying for. Getting this backwards is why so much footage never runs.
  2. Write hooks before anything else. The first two seconds decide whether the rest exists. We write ten and shoot the ones that survive being read aloud.
  3. Test cheap, scale what wins. Prove the concept at creator or performance tier, then spend properly behind the idea that already works.
  4. Shoot for multiple cuts. One session should produce vertical, square and landscape versions, not one landscape file that gets cropped badly later.
  5. Agree usage rights upfront. Paid advertising rights, darkposting and duration. Renegotiating on a performing ad is expensive.
  6. Deliver on a cadence. Fatigue arrives inside a fortnight, so a monthly batch beats a quarterly project every time.
  7. Retire on schedule. Replacing an ad before it collapses means you never run a decaying one while the next is in production.
  8. Report on concepts, not just spend. How many produced, how many tested, how many scaled. If creative volume drives performance, it belongs in the report.
