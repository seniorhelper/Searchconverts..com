Two completely different things are sold under this name

If you searched for YouTube promotion, roughly half the results will sell you views from a panel. The other half sell advertising. They are not variations of the same product, and one of them can cost you the channel.

Bought views and subscribers

Delivered by bots, proxy accounts or click farms. Cheap, fast, and prohibited.

  • YouTube's Fake Engagement policy bans anything that artificially inflates views, likes, comments or subscribers, whether by automated systems or by serving videos to unsuspecting viewers
  • The Terms of Service separately prohibit using automated means to interact with the platform
  • First violation typically brings a warning, and three strikes within 90 days can terminate a channel
  • Accounts identified as spam are removed and stop counting toward your totals, so the numbers you paid for disappear in periodic purges
  • Sub4sub arrangements count as artificial too, even between two willing humans

Actual YouTube advertising

Delivered through Google Ads and YouTube's own promotion tools. Slower, priced honestly, and legitimate.

  • YouTube explicitly treats views and subscribers gained through its own Promote product as legitimate
  • You pay per view rather than per impression on skippable formats, and if someone skips before the threshold you pay nothing
  • Targeting reaches people plausibly interested, so the retention signal that follows is real
  • Everything is reportable, auditable and reversible
  • Works alongside organic rather than against it

The part that should actually worry you

Forget policy for a moment. Bought engagement damages a channel even when it is never detected, and the mechanism is worth understanding because it is not obvious.

YouTube distributes video by predicting whether a given person will watch. It builds that prediction partly from how your existing audience behaves. Buy ten thousand subscribers who never watch anything, and you have told the algorithm that the people who know your channel best choose not to click. Your impressions then get served to an audience padded with accounts that never respond, click-through rate falls, average view duration falls, and distribution contracts.

You paid money to make your channel perform worse. That is the actual outcome, before any question of enforcement arises.

There is one more distinction almost nobody explains. Legitimate paid promotion is not the same as progress toward monetization. YouTube states that watch hours and subscribers gained through ad campaigns do not count toward Partner Program eligibility thresholds, even though those views are entirely legitimate. So advertising can grow a real audience and still not move the number you need for monetization. If someone sells you promotion as a route to the Partner Program, that is the sentence to ask them about.

We will not sell you views. Not as an upsell, not as a starter package, not quietly through a third party. If that is what you came for, the honest thing we can do is tell you what it does to a channel and let you spend the money elsewhere. Everything below is about the other kind.

Two things fall out of this that change how you would spend money.

The first is that click-through rate and retention multiply rather than add. A brilliant thumbnail on a video nobody finishes wastes impressions at scale, and YouTube learns from that. A superb video nobody clicks never gets the chance. Fixing whichever is weaker is worth more than improving the one that already works.

The second is that length is not free. Doubling a video's length while retention percentage holds does double watch time, which is why long-form still matters. But retention percentage almost never holds when you pad, so the practical version is: make it as long as it stays good and not one minute longer.

About the curve shape. The model draws a smooth decay fitted to the average percentage you set, because we cannot know where your particular video loses people. Your real retention graph in YouTube Studio will have a cliff in the first thirty seconds and probably a bump wherever something interesting happens. That shape is the most useful diagnostic you own, and reading three of your own graphs teaches you more than any general advice, including ours.
The demonstration

Same channel, two videos, and YouTube only pushes one

This is why buying views is not merely useless but counterproductive. Compare a video with a big view count and thin retention against one with a quarter of the views and real attention.

Watch hours produced, per video

Both are ten-minute videos. Video A has 40,000 views at 12% average viewed. Video B has 10,000 views at 55%.

Video A — views 40,000
Video B — views 10,000
Video A — watch hours 800
Video B — watch hours 917

Video A: 40,000 × 10 minutes × 12% = 800 hours. Video B: 10,000 × 10 minutes × 55% = 917 hours. Video B produced more watch time from a quarter of the audience, and it did so while signaling that people who click actually stay.

Now imagine Video A's extra thirty thousand views were purchased. They contribute almost no watch time, because bots do not watch. They drag the average percentage viewed down, which is the metric YouTube uses to predict whether the next person will stay. And they inflate the denominator on every engagement ratio you have.

The video looks more successful in public and performs worse in the system that decides who sees it next. That trade is bad in every direction, which is the honest reason we do not offer it rather than a moral one.

How a view is actually counted, and why it matters to your invoice

On skippable in-stream, a view registers when someone watches thirty seconds, or the whole ad if it is shorter, or interacts with it. Before that threshold, a skip costs you nothing.

That single rule changes how the creative should be built. Your first five seconds are not a hook competing for attention in the usual sense. They are a filter deciding who you pay for. A cold open that repels the wrong audience and holds the right one lowers your cost per acquisition while raising your cost per view, which looks worse on the report and is better for the business. This is why we treat cost per view as a diagnostic rather than a target.

The shift almost nobody has adjusted for

YouTube is now overwhelmingly a television. Roughly 60% of US watch time happens on TV screens against about 28% on mobile, and connected TV has grown to nearly a third of ad delivery from around a quarter a year earlier.

That reprices everything. Connected TV costs meaningfully more per view and delivers substantially better completion, and it produces almost no clicks because clicking is awkward on a television. If you blend connected TV into a single click-through-rate report you will conclude it is failing, then cut the surface where most of the watching happens. It should be judged on view rate, completion and brand lift, and segmented out of blended reporting. We cover the wider surface on our CTV advertising page.

The practical implication for creative is unglamorous: text has to be legible from a sofa, audio has to work without headphones, and an end screen asking someone to click is asking for something they cannot easily do.
