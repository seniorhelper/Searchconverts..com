Trust signals that actually increase conversions: what the evidence says, and the ones the FTC now fines

Search for trust signals and you will find the same list on every page, propped up by numbers that lead nowhere. We traced them. A few have solid research behind them, a few are invented, and one category, fake or bought reviews, now carries a federal penalty of $53,088 per violation.

What is a trust signal, and which ones have evidence behind them?

A trust signal is anything on a page that lowers the perceived risk of acting: a review, a price, a phone number, a guarantee, a seal, a real photo of the team. Visitors are asking one question before they buy, book or call: will this go the way I expect? Every trust signal is an answer to some part of it.

The research does not support every signal equally. Ranked by the strength of the public evidence we could verify:

Do reviews increase conversions?

Yes, and they are the best-documented trust signal there is. Northwestern University’s Spiegel Research Center found that the purchase likelihood for a product with five reviews is “270% greater” than for a product with none, and that the effect is bigger where the risk is bigger:

Three findings from the same research matter more for day-to-day decisions than the headline:

  • The first five reviews do most of the work. The benefit of more reviews diminishes “rapidly after the first five.” A page with zero reviews is the problem to solve first.
  • Perfect scores look suspicious. Purchase likelihood “typically peaks at ratings in the 4.0 – 4.7 range” and falls as ratings approach 5.0. A few honest four-star reviews help credibility; do not filter them out.
  • Verification helps. A verified-buyer label “improves the odds of purchase by 15%.” Showing where a review came from (Google, a booking system, a verified purchase) is a trust signal in itself.

For service businesses the higher-priced finding is the one to remember. A $12,000 roof or a $4,000 dental plan is exactly the kind of purchase where reviews carry the most weight.

How many visitors leave because they don’t trust the site?

Baymard Institute maintains the most-cited checkout research. Its documented average cart abandonment rate is 70.22%, a simple mean of 50 studies. When it asks US shoppers why they abandoned (excluding people who were just browsing), distrust is near the top:

Look at the list as a trust problem and more than one bar belongs to it. “Extra costs too high,” “couldn’t see total cost up front” and “returns policy not satisfactory” are all versions of “I wasn’t sure what I was agreeing to.” The fix for those is not a badge; it is showing the real price and terms early. Baymard does not publish its survey sample on the free page, so treat the percentages as directional.

Lead-generation sites have the same problem without a cart. A quote form that does not say what happens next, or a service page with no price range, leaves the same doubt; the visitor just leaves earlier.

Do trust badges and security seals actually work?

Sometimes, and far less dramatically than the internet claims. The search results for this question repeat figures like “a 42% lift from trust badges, per Baymard.” Baymard has no such finding; its well-known 42% is the share of shoppers who abandoned because they were “just browsing.” Other common figures (86%, up to 30%, 94%) trace to vendor marketing or nothing at all.

What the real evidence says:

  • A randomized field experiment found seals work. Özpolat, Gao, Jank and Viswanathan (Information Systems Research, 2013) ran a randomized experiment across 9,098 shopping sessions at an online retailer and reported “strong evidence” that displaying a third-party assurance seal increases purchase conversion. The public abstract does not give the effect size, and it is one retailer.
  • Recognition matters more than technology. Baymard’s 2013 survey of 2,510 US adults asked which seal gave the most sense of security; about half had no preference, and among the rest, familiar names won. Its conclusion: “what matters for the average user is the perceived security.” That survey measured perception, not conversion, and it is now over a decade old.

The practical reading: a seal or accreditation helps when it is recognizable to your audience, verifiable with one click, and placed where the risk is felt (the payment step, the quote form). A row of eight logos in the footer does very little.

What makes a website look credible?

Two pieces of research have held up for years. The Stanford/Consumer WebWatch study had 2,684 people evaluate the credibility of live websites; “design look” came up in 46.1% of their comments, more than any other factor. People judge credibility by how a site looks before they read a word.

Nielsen Norman Group’s summary of trustworthiness research names four factors:

  1. Design quality. “Typos, broken links, and other mistakes quickly degrade credibility.”
  2. Upfront disclosure. Be “upfront with all information that relates to the customer experience”: prices, fees, timelines, terms.
  3. Comprehensive, correct and current content. Show “the full range of services or products,” and keep it current.
  4. Connection to the rest of the web. People “trust these external sources more than company-sponsored content”: independent reviews, press, professional bodies.

Notice that three of the four have nothing to do with badges. An out-of-date copyright year, a service page that lists a service you stopped offering, or a “contact us for pricing” wall all cost trust quietly.

What do local customers check before they call?

For local service businesses, trust is mostly decided before the visit, on review platforms. BrightLocal’s Local Consumer Review Survey 2026 (1,002 US adults, February 2026) shows how high the bar is now:

The same survey found the share requiring 4.5 stars or more nearly doubled in a year (17% to 31%), that 50% are put off by templated owner replies, and that 97% think businesses should be punished for fake reviews. Customers also spread out: Google’s share as a review source fell from 83% to 71% while Apple Maps rose from 14% to 27%, and 45% now use AI tools for local recommendations. That means your website has to carry its own proof. A visitor arriving from an AI answer may never have seen your Google profile.

Which trust tactics are now illegal?

On August 14, 2024, the Federal Trade Commission voted 5-0 to finalize its Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials (16 CFR Part 465). It took effect October 21, 2024. In the FTC’s words, “Fake reviews not only waste people’s time and money, but also pollute the marketplace.” The rule prohibits:

  • Fake or false consumer or celebrity reviews and testimonials, including AI-generated ones.
  • Buying positive or negative reviews: incentives conditioned on the review’s sentiment.
  • Undisclosed reviews by company officers, managers or employees.
  • Company-controlled review sites presented as independent.
  • Review suppression through baseless legal threats, physical threats, intimidation or false accusations, and presenting a filtered review section as complete.
  • Buying or selling fake social media indicators such as bot followers or views.

Penalties apply to knowing violations. The FTC’s civil penalty is $53,088 per violation (raised from $51,744 in January 2025), and the FTC’s September 2026 Federal Register notice says amounts will remain unchanged during 2026. The FTC’s Endorsement Guides, revised in June 2023, already covered “procuring, suppressing, boosting, organizing, publishing, upvoting, downvoting, or editing consumer reviews,” and warn that a platform’s built-in disclosure tool “might not be an adequate disclosure.”

The conversion lesson: the trust signals that fail the law are the same ones that fail customers. Ninety-seven percent of consumers in BrightLocal’s survey want fake reviews punished. A real 4.6 with a few honest complaints and good replies outperforms a suspicious 5.0, according to Spiegel’s rating curve, and it carries no legal risk.

Where should trust signals go on a page?

The research above points to one placement rule: put the answer where the doubt appears. In practice, for most service and lead-generation pages:

  • Next to the main call to action: a rating with its count and source (“4.8 from 212 Google reviews”), linked so it can be checked.
  • Next to the form: what happens after submitting, how fast you reply, and that you will not share their details. This is where most lead forms lose people.
  • On pricing or quote pages: the guarantee or warranty in plain words, and a price range. “Contact us for pricing” is the opposite of a trust signal.
  • At payment: the seal or accreditation your audience recognizes, beside the card fields, plus the total with nothing added later.
  • On the about page: real people, real photos, licenses and accreditations with numbers that can be looked up.

Then test placement rather than assuming. Moving existing proof closer to the decision is one of the cheapest A/B tests to run, and our A/B testing page covers how long it needs to run.

    Questions people ask

    What are trust signals on a website?

    Elements that lower the perceived risk of acting: customer reviews, clear prices and terms, visible contact details, real team photos, guarantees, licenses and recognizable security or accreditation seals.

    Do trust badges increase conversion rates?

    They can. A randomized field experiment published in 2013 found a third-party assurance seal increased purchase conversion at an online retailer. Popular claims such as a 42% lift have no traceable source, and recognizable seals matter more than the technology behind them.

    How many reviews do I need before they help conversions?

    Spiegel Research Center found purchase likelihood with five reviews was 270% higher than with none, and that the benefit diminishes rapidly after the first five. Ratings between 4.0 and 4.7 converted best.

    Is it illegal to post fake reviews or testimonials?

    Yes. The FTC rule on consumer reviews and testimonials (16 CFR Part 465), effective October 21, 2024, bans fake and AI-generated reviews, buying sentiment-based reviews, undisclosed employee reviews and review suppression. Civil penalties are up to $53,088 per violation in 2026.

    Why do shoppers abandon checkout over trust?

    In Baymard Institute research, 19% of US shoppers who abandoned a checkout said they did not trust the site with their credit card information, and more cited unexpected costs or unclear totals.

    Can I offer customers an incentive for leaving a review?

    The FTC rule prohibits incentives conditioned on the review being positive or negative. Asking every customer for an honest review is fine; review platforms such as Google have their own stricter policies on incentives, so check them too.
