The commission is bigger than the margin

Median income before taxes at a full-service restaurant is 2.8% of sales. Delivery marketplaces take 15 to 30% of the ticket, and 25 to 35% once the fees stack. That is not a marketing problem you can out-post on social media. It is arithmetic.

Two things this model deliberately leaves out. Marketplaces genuinely do drive discovery, so a share of those orders are customers you would never have met. And running your own delivery has costs of its own. The honest position is the one most successful operators land on: marketplaces for discovery, your own channel for the people who already like you.

The part that compounds. The marketplace keeps the customer data. You do not get the name, the email or the order history, which means you cannot bring that guest back without paying the commission again. You are not buying an order. You are renting a customer, repeatedly, at a rate higher than your entire profit margin.
The most important page you own

What is your menu currently doing to be found?

Seventy-seven percent of diners look at a menu online before deciding where to eat. It is the most read thing your restaurant publishes. And in most restaurants it is a PDF, which means nobody except a human with a phone can read a word of it.

Search engines cannot extract dishes and prices from a PDF in any useful way. A PDF cannot carry structured data. It does not resize on a phone, so people pinch and zoom and give up. And when someone asks an AI assistant for a place nearby that does a good gluten-free pasta, your menu is not part of the answer, because as far as the machine is concerned your menu does not contain words.

Now every line is a fact a machine can use. Schema.org gives menus a proper vocabulary: Menu holds MenuSection, which holds MenuItem, which carries an Offer with a price and currency. Attach that to the Restaurant entity with your address, hours and coordinates and you have told Google and every AI assistant exactly what you serve, for how much, and to whom.

What structuring a menu actually gets you

A forty-item menu in plain HTML with proper markup creates dozens of new ways to be found. Every dish becomes a phrase someone might search. Every dietary tag becomes a filter you can appear in. Instead of competing only for the phrase your category name, you become findable for the specific thing a hungry person typed at six o'clock.

It also changes how you appear in AI answers. Assistants building a recommendation cross-reference structured data as a trust signal, because it is unambiguous in a way that prose is not. A restaurant that has declared its cuisine, hours, price range, service options and dish list in machine-readable form is far easier to recommend confidently than one that has published a picture of a menu.

One honest caveat, because plenty of agencies will not give you this one. Menu markup is not a guaranteed rich result in Google the way review stars are. Schema.org Menu is a vocabulary that improves understanding rather than a switch that produces a specific search feature. What it reliably does is make your dishes readable, make your entity coherent, and make you quotable to systems that are increasingly answering the question instead of listing links. That is worth doing on its own merits, and anyone promising you a guaranteed menu carousel is overselling.

Then we put the menu on their phone and leave it there

The second half is physical. We supply NFC stickers and cards for your tables, your counter and your takeout bags. A guest taps their phone against it, and your menu opens instantly. No app, no QR code to focus on in dim light, no typing.

The important part is what happens next. Once your menu is open on their phone, they can save it to their home screen in two taps. From that moment you are an icon on the device, sitting next to the apps they use every day, rather than a search they would have to remember to perform.

That is the whole game for a local restaurant. A guest who has your menu saved does not open a delivery marketplace and browse fifty competitors. They open you. And because the page is yours, you can put a phone number, a booking link and a direct order button on it, which means the next order costs you a card fee instead of a third of the ticket.

It also gives you something the marketplace will never hand over: a direct line. A saved menu is the local restaurant version of being on speed dial, and it is achieved with a sticker that costs less than one comped entree.

How people find dinner now

Three doors, and most restaurants only maintain one

A hungry person at six o'clock arrives through a map, an app, or an answer. Each one rewards something different, and only one of them is free.

Comparison of the three routes diners take to find a restaurant
The door What decides who wins What it costs you
The map Completeness and activity on your business profile, proximity, review volume and recency, plus how well your categories and attributes match the search Time, not commission
The app Which commission tier you bought, promotions you funded, and photography. Lower tiers are deprioritized in app search, which is how the pricing works 15–30% per order
The answer Whether a machine can read your menu, your hours, your location and your dietary options clearly enough to recommend you without hedging Structure, not spend

The third door is the one that changed recently and the one almost nobody has prepared for. When someone asks an assistant for somewhere nearby that is open late and does a good vegetarian main, the assistant needs facts it can trust. Restaurants that have published those facts in a readable form get named. Restaurants with a PDF and a Facebook page do not, and there is no bid you can place to fix it.

Your business profile still does an enormous amount of work and deserves genuine attention rather than a one-time setup: current hours including holidays, correct categories and attributes, photos added regularly, and replies to reviews. It is the cheapest asset in restaurant marketing and the most commonly neglected.

None of this means abandoning the apps. They are genuinely good at discovery, and a first-time guest found through a marketplace is still a guest you did not have. The mistake is treating a discovery channel as your main channel, because at 25 to 30% of the ticket the arithmetic at the top of this page never resolves in your favor.

Restaurant marketing questions, answered with numbers

A restaurant marketing agency helps fill covers and improve what each cover earns. In practice that splits into three jobs: being findable when someone is deciding where to eat, converting that attention into an order or a booking, and bringing guests back through a channel you own rather than one that charges commission. The part most owners underestimate is the margin side, because a restaurant can be busier and less profitable at the same time when volume arrives through a channel that takes 25 to 30% of the ticket.

Published 2026 rate cards put base commission at roughly 15 to 30% depending on tier, with pickup listed separately and lower. Most independents report the blended real cost landing between 25 and 35% once delivery, payment processing and marketing fees stack. Set that against the National Restaurant Association's median income before taxes of 2.8% of sales at full-service restaurants and 4.0% at limited-service, and the commission is several times larger than the entire profit margin. Lower commission tiers also buy less visibility inside the app, which is why most independents end up on the higher ones.
