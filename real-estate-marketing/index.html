The finding

The intent is already there. The follow-up is not.

Set two NAR findings next to each other and the whole industry's marketing problem appears in a single line.

More than nine out of ten buyers say they would use their agent again. Eighty-eight percent would use them again or recommend them. Eighty-seven percent of sellers would recommend theirs.

Now the other half. The median agent gets 28% of business from past clients and 22% from past-client referrals. That figure rose from 20% the year before, so it is improving. It is still nowhere near the intent that exists.

Put plainly: almost every client you have ever closed is willing to work with you again or send you someone. Most of them will not, because nobody asked and nobody stayed in touch. The relationship was earned and then abandoned.

This is why we treat a past-client database as a marketing channel rather than a mailing list. It is the only channel in real estate where the acquisition cost has already been paid.

Where business comes from, by years in the business

NAR's 2026 Member Profile. The gap between a new agent and a veteran is not talent. It is having a database and working it.

The uncomfortable implication for a new agent. If half of a veteran's business comes from a database you do not have yet, then your first two years are not a marketing problem you can outspend. They are a database-building problem. Every transaction you close is an asset, and the way you treat it after closing decides whether you are still buying leads in year ten.
The second finding

Most clients interview exactly one agent

Seventy-seven percent of repeat buyers interview only one agent before deciding. Around seven in ten sellers do the same. There is no competitive pitch, because there is usually no competition in the room.

That single fact rearranges everything about how real estate marketing should work.

It means the decision is made before the appointment. By the time someone calls you, they have already chosen. Your listing presentation is not winning the business, it is confirming a choice made earlier, usually from a referral or a prior relationship.

Forty-three percent of buyers found their agent through a referral. Eighteen percent used an agent they had worked with before. For sellers, 66% came through a referral or a past agent, and 35% named the agent's reputation as the deciding factor.

So the marketing that matters happens months or years before the transaction, in front of people who are not currently buying or selling anything. That is a genuinely different job from generating leads, and most agents only budget for the second one.

It also explains why speed matters so much on the leads you do buy. If someone is contacting agents cold, they are in comparison mode, and comparison mode is decided by who answers first.

The part nobody wants to hear about response time

Speed to contact is the most replicated finding in sales research, and real estate is the clearest case of it. Contact a new inquiry within five minutes and you are dramatically more likely to qualify them than an agent who waits half an hour. Wait an hour and the person is usually already speaking to someone else.

Meanwhile the average agent takes many hours to respond to an online lead, and gives up after roughly two attempts. Published guidance is consistent that most sales need five or more follow-up contacts.

This is why we will not sell an agent more leads until the response system exists. A $200 lead with no five-minute reply and no follow-up cadence is $200 set on fire, and buying more of them just increases the burn rate. Fixing response is conversion optimization applied to a phone rather than a page, and it costs a fraction of the media it rescues.

Context

The market you are actually selling into

Three shifts in the NAR data change who your marketing should be talking to.

First-time buyers have collapsed as a share of the market

They fell to 24% of buyers, the lowest share since NAR began collecting the data in 1981, down from 32% the prior year. Affordability is the leading barrier that brokerages report, at 27%, well ahead of low inventory at 12%.

If your entire content strategy is first-time buyer guides, you are competing hardest for the shrinking end of the market.

The equity-rich buyer is doing the transacting

Twenty-six percent of buyers paid all cash, an all-time high, and the median down payment climbed to 23%. Repeat buyers with equity can move relatively easily while first-time buyers struggle to save. That is two different audiences needing two different messages, and most agent marketing speaks only to one.

Agents are not leaving, they are consolidating

Seventy-five percent of members are very certain they will stay in real estate for at least two more years. The typical member now has 13 years of experience. Fifteen percent have two years or less while 23% have 26 years or more, and that split keeps widening.

So the competition is not thinning out. It is getting more experienced, and the experienced end is the end with the databases. That is the strategic case for building yours now rather than after the market turns.

And the agent's role is not in question

For-sale-by-owner transactions sit at 5%, an all-time low. Ninety-one percent of sellers used an agent and 88% of buyers purchased through one. FSBO homes sold at a median of $360,000 against $425,000 for agent-assisted sales. Whatever pressure the industry is under, consumers are still overwhelmingly choosing to be represented.

Sources: NAR 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers, and NAR 2026 Member Profile, both national surveys with published methodology. Lead conversion and cost figures elsewhere on this page come from industry vendor reporting and are shown as ranges for that reason.
